About Courtenay

Courtenay is located on the eastern coast of Vancouver Island, roughly 220 kilometres north of Victoria and approximately 75 kilometres north of Nanaimo, making it one of the largest urban centres in the northern portion of the island. Situated within the Comox Valley, the city is framed by the Beaufort Mountain Range to the west, the Strait of Georgia to the east, and the Courtenay River flowing through its core. The surrounding landscape includes fertile agricultural land, forested foothills, and easy access to both alpine and coastal environments. Courtenay enjoys a relatively mild, four-season climate, with warm, dry summers and cool, wet winters, though its proximity to the mountains means snowfall is accessible nearby at higher elevations. Courtenay is a mid-sized city with a population of approximately 30,000 residents, though the broader Comox Valley regional population is considerably larger when neighbouring communities are included. The city carries a distinct blend of small-town warmth and growing urban amenities, appealing to long-time island residents and newcomers alike. Its downtown core features heritage architecture alongside contemporary development, and the riverfront areas contribute to a welcoming, walkable character. The community has a strong arts and culture presence, supported by local galleries, performing arts facilities, and seasonal festivals that reflect the valley's creative and outdoor-oriented identity. Proximity to Vancouver Island's natural attractions, including ski hills, hiking trails, beaches, and marine environments, shapes much of the daily lifestyle available to residents. Courtenay's economy is anchored by a combination of public sector employment, healthcare services, retail trade, education, and tourism. The nearby Canadian Armed Forces base in the adjacent community of Comox plays a significant role in the regional economy, supporting a steady population of military personnel and their families. Agriculture remains part of the valley's economic fabric, with local farms contributing to both food production and agritourism. The city serves as a regional service hub for communities throughout the northern Vancouver Island corridor, meaning a broad range of shopping, dining, healthcare, and professional services are available locally. Tourism continues to grow as an economic driver, fuelled by outdoor recreation, coastal access, and the valley's natural beauty.
