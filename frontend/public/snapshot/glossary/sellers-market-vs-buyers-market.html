Buying & Selling

Sellers Market vs Buyers Market

A sellers' market is a market condition in which the number of buyers exceeds the supply of homes for sale, characterized by shorter days on market, sale prices at or above list price, multiple offers on a typical listing, and reduced use of buyer-side conditions. A buyers' market is the inverse condition, in which homes-for-sale supply exceeds active buyer demand, characterized by longer days on market, sale prices below list, fewer competing offers, and greater use of buyer-side conditions and price negotiation. A balanced market is the intermediate condition in which supply and demand are roughly matched. Market type is commonly assessed using the sales-to-active-listings ratio published by BC real estate boards.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do BC real estate boards determine whether a market is a sellers' market, buyers' market, or balanced market in British Columbia?

BC real estate boards, such as the Greater Vancouver Realtors and the BC Real Estate Association, assess market conditions primarily using the sales-to-active-listings ratio, which compares the number of completed sales in a period against the total number of active listings. Generally, a higher ratio indicates seller-side pressure while a lower ratio indicates buyer-side pressure, with a middle range representing balanced conditions. The exact threshold ranges used can shift over time, so consulting the monthly statistics packages published directly by the relevant BC real estate board provides the most current benchmarks.

In a BC sellers' market, are licensees still required to disclose multiple offers to all competing buyers?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules administered by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), a listing licensee must promptly disclose to all competing offerors the existence of other offers, though the terms of those offers are kept confidential. This obligation applies regardless of market conditions, meaning the multiple-offer situations common in a sellers' market do not relieve a licensee of their disclosure duties. Licensees must also act honestly and in good faith toward all parties under RESA.

Verify with: BC Real Estate Association
How does a sellers' market in BC affect the use of subject-to-financing or inspection conditions in purchase contracts?

In a BC sellers' market, intense buyer competition often leads purchasers to reduce or remove subject conditions — such as subject-to-financing or subject-to-inspection clauses — in order to make their offers more attractive. The BCFSA has published guidance cautioning buyers about the risks of waiving conditions without fully understanding their implications, as removing a subject clause means the contract becomes immediately binding upon acceptance. Buyers considering removing conditions should weigh those risks carefully before doing so.

Does the BC Property Transfer Tax change or have different exemptions depending on whether it is a sellers' or buyers' market?

The Property Transfer Tax Act does not adjust its rates or exemption thresholds based on market conditions. As of 2026, the general PTT rates are 1% on the first $200,000 of fair market value, 2% on the portion between $200,000 and $3,000,000, 3% on the portion above $3,000,000, and an additional 2% on the residential portion above $3,000,000. Exemptions such as the First-Time Home Buyers' Program (full exemption up to $835,000) and the Newly Built Home Exemption (up to $1,100,000) apply based on eligibility criteria set out in the Property Transfer Tax Act, not on prevailing market conditions.

In a BC buyers' market, can a buyer negotiate for the seller to pay the buyer's licensee's remuneration, and how is this governed?

Remuneration arrangements in BC are governed by the Real Estate Services Act and BCFSA rules, which require that all remuneration agreements be in writing and fully disclosed to the client. In a buyers' market, there may be more room to negotiate contract terms, including who covers a buyer's licensee's fee, but any such arrangement must comply with RESA's disclosure and written agreement requirements. Licensees are prohibited from accepting undisclosed remuneration, regardless of market conditions.

How do sellers' and buyers' market conditions affect strata property sales in BC, and are there unique strata disclosure obligations regardless of market type?

Whether BC is in a sellers' or buyers' market, the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) imposes consistent disclosure obligations on sellers of strata lots, including providing documents such as the Form B Information Certificate, Form F Certificate of Payment, current bylaws, rules, and the most recent depreciation report if one exists. A buyers' market may give purchasers more time and leverage to review strata documents carefully before removing subject conditions, while a sellers' market may compress that review window. Regardless of market conditions, buyers retain the legal right to obtain these documents under the Strata Property Act.

Verify with: BC Real Estate Association
Can a BC licensee use aggressive or high-pressure marketing tactics during a sellers' market, such as sending unsolicited promotional emails to prospective buyers?

Sending unsolicited commercial electronic messages — such as promotional emails to prospective buyers without their express or implied consent — is regulated by Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL), a federal statute that applies to all commercial actors in BC including real estate licensees. Additionally, the collection, use, and disclosure of personal information by BC licensees is governed by the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), which requires consent for the collection and use of personal data. A sellers' market does not create any exemption from these obligations.

If a BC property is being sold by an executor or administrator of an estate during a buyers' market, are there special considerations that apply?

When a property is sold as part of a deceased person's estate in BC, the executor or administrator derives authority to sell from the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA) and from any grant of probate or administration issued by the BC Supreme Court. In a buyers' market, an executor may face longer selling timelines and lower offers, but their fiduciary duty to the estate beneficiaries requires them to act in the best interests of the estate, which generally means seeking fair market value. Any sale by an executor should be conducted in compliance with both WESA and RESA if a licensee is engaged.

Verify with: BC Real Estate Association
How does a sellers' market in BC interact with Agricultural Land Reserve properties, and can sellers of ALR land command premium prices for subdivision potential?

Land within BC's Agricultural Land Reserve is subject to the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), administered by the Agricultural Land Commission (ALC), which places significant restrictions on subdivision, non-farm use, and non-adhering residential uses regardless of prevailing market conditions. Even in a strong sellers' market, a property's location within the ALR does not permit subdivision or non-farm development without ALC approval, which is not guaranteed, and representations about subdivision potential for ALR land must be made accurately and with supporting evidence. Licensees who misrepresent ALR development potential may be in breach of their duties under RESA.

Verify with: BC Real Estate Association
In a BC buyers' market, what recourse does a seller have if a buyer fails to complete the purchase after all subjects have been removed?

Once all subject conditions in a BC real estate contract have been waived or removed, the contract becomes a binding agreement, and a buyer who fails to complete exposes themselves to a claim by the seller for damages for breach of contract, which may include forfeiture of the deposit and a claim for any loss on a lower subsequent sale. Dispute resolution options include civil litigation in BC courts, and parties may also attempt to resolve disputes through the BC Civil Resolution Tribunal depending on the amount in issue. Where a licensee's conduct is at issue, a complaint may be filed with the BCFSA under RESA.
