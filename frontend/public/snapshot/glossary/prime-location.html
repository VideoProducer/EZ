Property Types

Prime location

A neighbourhood or street considered highly desirable due to reputation, proximity to amenities, schools, transport, and low density.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does BC law require a seller's licensee to disclose that a property is in a 'prime location' when marketing it?

There is no specific statutory duty under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) or its Rules to characterise a neighbourhood as 'prime location,' but licensees are prohibited from making false or misleading representations under RESA and BCFSA conduct standards. Describing a location as 'prime' must therefore be supportable by objective factors such as proximity to amenities, school catchments, and transit, rather than being an unsubstantiated marketing claim. BCFSA guidance on advertising accuracy applies to all promotional statements made by licensees.

Verify with: Land Title Act (RSBC 1996, c. 250) · BC Assessment
Can a property in BC's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) ever qualify as a 'prime location' for residential development purposes?

ALR land is subject to strict use restrictions under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), administered by the Agricultural Land Commission (ALC), which limits non-farm use and residential subdivision regardless of neighbourhood desirability. A parcel's perceived locational prestige does not override ALC approval requirements for non-farm use or exclusion from the ALR. Buyers interested in ALR properties should consult the ALC directly for current restrictions on residential and non-farm uses.

How does being in a 'prime location' affect the Property Transfer Tax payable on a BC residential purchase?

The BC Property Transfer Tax Act applies regardless of neighbourhood desirability; PTT is calculated on the fair market value of the property at rates of 1% on the first $200,000, 2% on the portion from $200,001 to $2,000,000, 3% on the portion from $2,000,001 to $3,000,000, and an additional 2% on the residential portion exceeding $3,000,000. Prime-location properties often command higher prices, which can push purchasers into higher PTT tiers or disqualify them from exemptions such as the First-Time Home Buyers' Program (full exemption up to $835,000) or the Newly Built Home Exemption (up to $1,100,000). Consult the BC Ministry of Finance for current thresholds and eligibility criteria.

If a strata lot is marketed as being in a 'prime location,' what strata documents should a buyer review before completing the purchase?

Under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43), buyers should review the Form B Information Certificate, which discloses the strata corporation's financial standing, bylaws, and special levies, as well as the most recent depreciation report and the contingency reserve fund balance. A desirable location does not diminish the importance of understanding a strata corporation's fiscal health and bylaw restrictions, which can significantly affect the enjoyment and value of the unit. These documents are legally required to be provided by the strata corporation upon request.

Does BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) limit how a licensee may collect and use buyer data gathered during a 'prime location' property marketing campaign?

Yes, under BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), licensees must collect personal information only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate, must obtain meaningful consent, and must limit use to those disclosed purposes. Running targeted marketing campaigns for prime-location listings that involve collecting buyer contact details requires clear disclosure of purpose and proper consent. Additionally, any unsolicited commercial electronic messages sent as part of such campaigns must comply with Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL), including express or implied consent and an unsubscribe mechanism.

How does a property's 'prime location' affect its assessed value for BC property tax purposes?

BC Assessment determines a property's assessed value based on market value as of July 1 of the preceding year, and properties in prime locations typically attract higher assessed values because comparable sales in desirable neighbourhoods support higher valuations. The assessed value forms the basis for municipal and provincial property taxes, meaning owners in prime locations generally pay higher property taxes. Property owners who believe their assessment is inaccurate may file a complaint with BC Assessment's Property Assessment Review Panel within the prescribed deadline.

Can a licensee in BC use 'prime location' claims in online advertising without restriction?

Under RESA and BCFSA's advertising rules, all real estate advertising must be accurate, not misleading, and must clearly identify the responsible licensee and brokerage. Using 'prime location' as an advertising claim is permissible provided it is truthful and supported by verifiable attributes of the property's surroundings, but licensees must not make exaggerated or unsubstantiated claims. BCFSA may investigate complaints about misleading advertising, and breaches can result in disciplinary action under RESA.

If a BC estate property is situated in a prime location, does the executor have any special obligations related to its sale?

Under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA), an executor has a fiduciary duty to administer the estate in the best interests of the beneficiaries, which includes obtaining fair market value when selling estate real property, whether or not it is in a prime location. The premium value associated with a prime location may make it especially important for the executor to obtain a professional appraisal and, where required, court approval for the sale, to demonstrate the estate received proper value. Executors should seek independent legal advice to ensure compliance with WESA obligations.

Does 'prime location' status protect a BC property from being affected by nearby rezoning or development?

No, a neighbourhood's current reputation as a prime location does not create any legal protection against future rezoning, densification, or development approvals under BC's Local Government Act or Vancouver Charter, as applicable. Municipal and regional governments retain broad powers to amend Official Community Plans and zoning bylaws, which can alter neighbourhood character regardless of existing desirability. Buyers should review the current Official Community Plan and any proposed land-use amendments with the relevant local government before purchasing.

Verify with: Land Title Act (RSBC 1996, c. 250) · BC Assessment
Is a buyer's agent in BC obligated to conduct due diligence on a property's 'prime location' claims on behalf of their client?

Under RESA and BCFSA's standards for licensee conduct, a buyer's agent owes a duty of loyalty and competent service to their client, which includes taking reasonable steps to verify material representations about a property, including locational claims. While a licensee is not an expert in urban planning or infrastructure, they should assist clients in accessing publicly available information such as school catchment boundaries, transit authority data, and municipal amenity maps. If a prime-location claim appears to be a material factor in the client's decision, the licensee should encourage the client to independently verify those factors.
