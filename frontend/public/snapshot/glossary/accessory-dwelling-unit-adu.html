Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU)

A self-contained secondary residential unit on the same lot as a principal residence, including secondary suites, laneway houses, garden suites, and coach houses. Recent BC housing legislation requires most municipalities to permit one or more ADUs on lots zoned for single-family use, subject to local bylaws.

Frequently Asked Questions

What legislation requires BC municipalities to permit ADUs on single-family zoned lots?

The BC government introduced amendments through the Housing Statutes (Residential Development) Amendment Act, 2023, which amended the Local Government Act to require most municipalities to allow small-scale multi-unit housing, including secondary suites and laneway houses, on lots zoned for single-family and duplex use. This legislation was designed to come into full effect by mid-2024, with municipalities required to update their zoning bylaws accordingly. Individual municipalities still regulate specifics such as setbacks, maximum unit size, and parking through their own bylaws within the provincial framework.

Does a secondary suite inside a principal residence qualify as an ADU in BC?

Yes, a secondary suite contained within the principal dwelling — such as a basement suite — is one of the most common forms of ADU recognized in BC, alongside detached structures such as laneway houses, garden suites, and coach houses. Each municipality's zoning bylaw defines the specific development standards that apply, including ceiling height minimums, separate entrance requirements, and maximum floor area. Buyers and sellers should review the relevant local government bylaw to confirm whether an existing secondary suite was constructed with a valid permit and meets current standards.

How does the presence of an ADU affect the Property Transfer Tax (PTT) payable when purchasing a property in BC?

Under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act, PTT is calculated on the fair market value of the entire property transferred, including any ADU, at the standard rates of 1% on the first $200,000, 2% on the portion between $200,000 and $2,000,000, 3% on the portion between $2,000,000 and $3,000,000, and a further 2% on any residential value exceeding $3,000,000. A newly built ADU that forms part of a newly built home may qualify for the Newly Built Home Exemption if the total fair market value does not exceed $1,100,000, subject to eligibility conditions. Consult the BC Ministry of Finance or a tax professional for confirmation of the exemption's applicability to your specific transaction.

Can an ADU be built on land within BC's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR)?

Land within BC's Agricultural Land Reserve is subject to the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36) and the Land Reserve Protection Act, which significantly restrict non-farm residential use. The Agricultural Land Commission's regulations limit the number and size of residences permitted on ALR parcels, and proposals for additional residential units beyond what is permitted as a principal farm residence may require a non-farm-use application to the ALC. Property owners with ALR land should consult the Agricultural Land Commission directly for current rules applicable to their parcel and zone.

What disclosure obligations does a licensed real estate professional have when listing a property with an ADU in BC?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA's rules, a licensee must disclose all known material latent defects to buyers, which can include an unpermitted ADU or one that does not comply with the BC Building Code or local bylaws. A licensee acting as a buyer's agent also has a duty to take reasonable steps to discover material information about the property, including verifying whether the ADU has a valid building permit and occupancy approval. BCFSA provides professional standards guidance, and licensees should conduct appropriate due diligence rather than relying solely on seller representations.

Can a strata lot in BC have an ADU?

Strata lots are governed by the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43), and the ability to add or use an ADU within a strata lot depends on both the strata corporation's bylaws and the applicable municipal zoning. Many strata bylaws restrict or prohibit rental of any portion of a strata lot, subdividing a lot, or making structural alterations without strata council or general meeting approval. An owner wishing to create an ADU within their strata lot should review the strata corporation's bylaws and seek any required strata approval, in addition to obtaining municipal permits.

Does renting out an ADU trigger any privacy obligations for the landlord under BC law?

If a landlord collects, uses, or discloses personal information about prospective or current tenants — such as during a rental application process — the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) of BC applies to that activity if conducted in the course of a commercial enterprise. PIPA requires that personal information be collected with consent, used only for the purpose for which it was collected, and stored securely. Landlords who send electronic communications to prospective tenants should also be aware that Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) may apply to unsolicited commercial electronic messages.

What happens to an ADU on a property that forms part of a deceased owner's estate in BC?

When a property owner in BC dies, their real property — including any ADU on the lot — passes according to their will or, if they died intestate, under the distribution rules in the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA). The executor or administrator of the estate is responsible for managing the property, which may include complying with existing tenancy agreements affecting the ADU under the Residential Tenancy Act. Transfer of the property to a beneficiary or through a sale by the estate may trigger Property Transfer Tax obligations unless a qualifying exemption applies under the Property Transfer Tax Act.

Are there BC-wide size or height limits that apply to all ADUs?

The BC government has set provincial baseline standards under the Small-Scale Multi-Unit Housing legislation, including minimum allowable floor areas for secondary suites and detached ADUs, but individual municipalities may adopt standards that are more permissive within the provincial framework. Specific maximum floor area ratios, setbacks, and height limits are established by each local government's zoning bylaw and may vary significantly between jurisdictions. Consult the current BC Government guidance and the relevant municipal bylaw for precise dimensional standards applicable to a specific lot.

Can a homeowner in BC stratify an ADU to sell it as a separate legal unit?

Creating a separate stratified unit from an ADU requires approval under both the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) and the applicable municipal subdivision or zoning rules, and is generally a complex process that is distinct from simply operating an ADU as a rental. Bare land strata or building strata conversions are subject to local government approval and the provisions of the Strata Property Act governing the creation of new strata corporations. Owners considering stratification of an ADU should obtain legal advice and consult both the local government and a licensed BC real estate professional familiar with strata creation processes.
