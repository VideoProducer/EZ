Inspection & Home Condition

Foundation Issues

Foundation issues include cracks (vertical hairline vs horizontal/stepped), settling, water intrusion, bowing walls, and insufficient drainage. Common in older BC homes built on clay or fill. Most cosmetic cracks are harmless; structural cracks typically require a structural engineer's report and remediation costs of $10,000–$100,000+. Foundation concerns flagged in a home-inspection report are commonly escalated to a structural engineer before subject removal on the inspection condition.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a seller in British Columbia have a legal duty to disclose known foundation issues to a buyer?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA conduct rules, a licensee acting for a seller must disclose all known material latent defects — including structural foundation problems that are not visible on a reasonable inspection — to prospective buyers. A seller who knowingly conceals a material defect may face civil liability for misrepresentation regardless of whether a licensee is involved. Foundation issues that affect the structural integrity or habitability of a home are widely considered material latent defects in BC practice.

What is the difference between a cosmetic and a structural foundation crack in a BC home, and why does it matter?

Vertical hairline cracks in concrete foundations are commonly caused by normal curing shrinkage and are generally considered cosmetic, while horizontal cracks, stepped diagonal cracks in block or brick, or cracks accompanied by bowing walls typically indicate structural movement and are treated as serious concerns. The distinction matters because structural cracks usually require assessment by a licensed structural engineer before a buyer can accurately evaluate risk or cost of remediation, which in BC can range from roughly $10,000 to over $100,000 depending on severity. Identifying the crack type correctly determines whether remediation is minor patching or major underpinning.

How do foundation issues affect the inspection condition in a BC Contract of Purchase and Sale?

In BC, a buyer commonly includes a subject-to-inspection condition in the Contract of Purchase and Sale, giving them a defined period to have the property inspected before the contract becomes firm. If a home inspector flags foundation concerns during that period, buyers typically escalate the matter by retaining a licensed structural engineer for a more detailed assessment before deciding whether to remove the inspection subject or negotiate with the seller. The buyer's right to act on such findings is governed by the terms of the contract itself, so the wording of the condition and its deadline are important.

Who regulates home inspectors in British Columbia, and can a home inspector determine whether a foundation crack is structural?

Home inspectors in BC are regulated under the Home Inspector Licensing Regulation, administered by the BCFSA. While a licensed home inspector can visually identify and flag potential foundation concerns, they are not structural engineers and are not qualified to provide a structural assessment or render an opinion on engineering remediation — they will typically recommend further evaluation by a licensed professional engineer (P.Eng.) in BC when structural issues are suspected.

If a strata lot in BC has foundation issues, who is responsible for repairs — the strata owner or the strata corporation?

Responsibility depends on whether the affected area is a strata lot, common property, or limited common property as defined under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43). Foundations are almost always part of the common property or the building structure, meaning the strata corporation is generally responsible for repair and maintenance under the Strata Property Act, with costs typically drawn from the contingency reserve fund or funded through a special levy approved by the owners. Buyers reviewing a strata purchase should request the Form B Information Certificate and current depreciation report to understand the strata corporation's reserve fund position and any outstanding structural issues.

Can foundation issues affect how Property Transfer Tax is calculated on a BC home purchase?

Property Transfer Tax (PTT) under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act is calculated based on the fair market value of the property at the time of transfer, not on the physical condition or cost of repairs. However, if serious foundation issues have materially reduced the agreed purchase price below typical market value, the BC Ministry of Finance may still assess PTT on fair market value rather than the contract price, so buyers should be aware that a negotiated price reduction does not automatically reduce the PTT base. Buyers seeking exemptions such as the First-Time Home Buyer Program or Newly Built Home exemption should consult current BC Ministry of Finance guidance for applicable thresholds.

Are older BC homes particularly susceptible to foundation issues, and what soil conditions increase the risk?

Yes — older BC homes, particularly those built before modern building codes required engineered footings and adequate drainage, are more commonly associated with foundation movement. Homes built on expansive clay soils, organic fill, or poorly compacted land (conditions found in parts of the Lower Mainland, Fraser Valley, and interior BC) are especially prone to differential settling, heaving, and water intrusion because these soils shift significantly with changes in moisture content. A pre-purchase soil or geotechnical assessment may be warranted for properties in known high-risk areas.

What role does a structural engineer's report play when a BC buyer discovers foundation issues before subject removal?

A structural engineer licensed as a Professional Engineer (P.Eng.) in BC can assess the cause and severity of foundation damage, provide an opinion on remediation options, and estimate associated costs — information a buyer needs to make an informed decision before removing the inspection subject. The report may also be required by lenders or insurers before financing or property insurance can be secured on a property with known structural concerns. If the report identifies serious deficiencies, a buyer may use it to renegotiate price, request remediation as a condition of closing, or exercise any contractual right to rescind if the condition permits.

Under RESA and BCFSA rules, what are a buyer's agent's obligations when foundation issues are discovered during due diligence?

A licensee representing a buyer in BC owes a duty of loyalty and must act in the buyer's best interests under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the BCFSA Rules. This includes ensuring the buyer is aware of the significance of flagged foundation concerns, facilitating access to qualified experts such as structural engineers within the inspection period, and communicating all relevant findings clearly so the buyer can make an informed decision about proceeding, renegotiating, or withdrawing. A licensee must not minimize or suppress information about defects in a manner that could harm the client's interests.

Can foundation issues on an ALR property in BC create additional complications beyond the structural concern itself?

If a property is within BC's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR), administered by the Agricultural Land Commission under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), any proposed remediation or reconstruction work — such as underpinning, drainage improvements, or demolition and rebuilding of a residence — may require ALC approval depending on the nature and scope of the work and how it affects permitted land uses. Buyers of ALR properties with foundation concerns should confirm with the ALC and the relevant local government whether permits or approvals are needed before undertaking remediation, as non-compliant improvements on ALR land can create legal and resale complications.
