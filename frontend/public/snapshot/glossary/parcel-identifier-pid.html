Parcel Identifier (PID)

A Parcel Identifier (PID) is a unique nine-digit number assigned to each parcel of land registered in British Columbia's land title system, as administered by the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250. Because civic addresses can change due to municipal renumbering or development, the PID provides a stable, authoritative reference for identifying a specific parcel across title searches, property transfers, and registration documents. Buyers, lawyers, and notaries routinely use the PID to confirm they are dealing with the correct parcel before completing a transaction. Verify current administrative details regarding PID assignment and search procedures with the Land Title and Survey Authority or a BC lawyer or notary.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Parcel Identifier (PID) in British Columbia?

A Parcel Identifier (PID) is a unique 9-digit number assigned by the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC (LTSA) to every parcel of land registered in the BC land title system under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250. The PID is the most reliable way to identify a specific property because civic addresses can change due to municipal renumbering or subdivision. The PID remains constant as long as the legal boundaries of the parcel do not change.

Where can I find the PID for a BC property?

The PID appears on official documents from the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC, including the Certificate of Indefeasible Title (formerly 'title certificate'), title search results, and property assessment notices from BC Assessment. Real estate listings often include the PID, and licensed BC real estate professionals access it through Multiple Listing Service (MLS®) records. For authoritative confirmation, verify the PID by ordering a title search from the LTSA (www.ltsa.ca) or through a BC lawyer or notary public.

Verify with: LTSA — Glossary of Common Terms
Is a PID the same as a legal description or civic address?

No. The PID is a unique 9-digit administrative identifier assigned under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250, whereas the legal description (e.g., 'Lot 12, District Lot 123, Plan 4567') is the surveyed land description that defines the parcel's boundaries. The civic address (e.g., '123 Main Street, Vancouver, BC') is assigned by the local municipality under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, and can change; the PID and legal description remain stable unless the parcel boundaries are altered through subdivision or consolidation.

Do I need the PID to file Property Transfer Tax in BC?

Yes. The Property Transfer Tax Return (Form PTT-005) filed under the Property Transfer Tax Act, RSBC 1996, c. 378, requires the PID to identify the property being transferred. The PID ensures the BC Ministry of Finance correctly registers the transaction and applies any exemptions (e.g., First-Time Home Buyer Exemption) to the correct parcel. Verify the PID matches the title documents before filing; errors can delay registration or result in tax reassessment.

Verify with: LTSA — Glossary of Common Terms
Can a single property have more than one PID?

Yes. If a property comprises multiple separate legal parcels (e.g., a house and adjacent lot never consolidated), each parcel has its own PID under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250. Strata lots (condominiums, townhouses) each have a distinct PID, even within the same strata plan, as governed by the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43. When buying or selling, confirm all PIDs included in the transaction with a BC lawyer or notary to avoid inadvertently omitting a parcel.

Verify with: LTSA — Glossary of Common Terms
How does the PID relate to strata properties in BC?

Each strata lot in a strata plan registered under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, receives its own unique PID from the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC. The strata lot PID appears on the individual strata lot's Certificate of Indefeasible Title and identifies only that unit (e.g., 'Strata Lot 45, Plan LMS1234'), not the entire strata complex. The strata corporation may hold additional PIDs for common property parcels (e.g., parking areas, amenity land) if those were legally segregated.

Does the PID change if I subdivide my property in BC?

Yes. Subdivision creates new legal parcels, each receiving a new PID from the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC once the subdivision plan is registered under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250. The original PID is cancelled and replaced by the PIDs of the newly created lots. Subdivision requires municipal approval under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, and compliance with zoning bylaws; consult a BC land surveyor, lawyer, or notary before proceeding.

Is the PID used for property tax and assessment purposes in BC?

Yes. BC Assessment uses the PID to identify each property for annual assessed value determinations under the Assessment Act, RSBC 1996, c. 20, and municipalities use it to administer property taxes under the Community Charter, SBC 2003, c. 26, and Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. The PID appears on your annual BC Assessment Notice and municipal property tax notice. If you believe the PID on a tax notice is incorrect, contact BC Assessment or your municipal tax office immediately to correct the record.

Can I search BC land title records using only the PID?

Yes. The Land Title and Survey Authority of BC (www.ltsa.ca) allows title searches by PID under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250. A PID search retrieves the current Certificate of Indefeasible Title, registered charges (mortgages, liens, covenants), and legal description for that specific parcel. BC lawyers and notaries routinely search by PID during conveyancing to ensure clear title; you may also order a title search directly from the LTSA for a fee (verify current fee schedule at www.ltsa.ca).

Verify with: LTSA — Glossary of Common Terms
What should I do if the PID on my title document doesn't match my assessment notice?

Contact both the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC (www.ltsa.ca) and BC Assessment (www.bcassessment.ca) immediately to determine which record is correct under the Land Title Act, RSBC 1996, c. 250, and Assessment Act, RSBC 1996, c. 20. A mismatch may indicate a clerical error, an unregistered subdivision, or confusion between merged or consolidated parcels. Verify the correct PID with a BC lawyer or notary before signing any real estate contract or tax appeal to avoid legal complications or incorrect property tax liability.
