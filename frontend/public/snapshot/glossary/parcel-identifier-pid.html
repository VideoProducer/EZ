Parcel Identifier (PID)

A unique 9-digit identifier assigned to every parcel of land registered at the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC. The PID is the most reliable way to identify a specific property, since civic addresses can change.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Parcel Identifier (PID) in British Columbia?

A Parcel Identifier (PID) is a unique 9-digit number assigned by the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC (LTSA) to every parcel of land registered in the BC land title system. It serves as the definitive identifier for a specific parcel, remaining constant even if the civic address, owner, or zoning changes. Because civic addresses can be reassigned or duplicated, the PID is the most reliable reference when searching for or describing a property in BC.

Who assigns a PID to a property in British Columbia?

The Land Title and Survey Authority of BC (LTSA) assigns a PID when a parcel is first registered in the BC land title system, typically upon subdivision, consolidation, or the initial registration of a new legal parcel. The LTSA administers BC's land title registry under the Land Title Act (RSBC 1996, c. 250), which governs the registration of interests in land throughout the province. A PID is not issued by a municipality or any federal body — it originates solely from the LTSA.

How can I look up a property's PID in British Columbia?

PIDs can be searched through the LTSA's myLTSA portal, where users can conduct title searches using a civic address, legal description, or an existing PID. The BC Assessment Authority's property search tool also displays PIDs alongside assessment information for most properties in the province. Real estate licensees regulated by the BCFSA under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) routinely use the LTSA portal to retrieve accurate PID information when preparing contracts or conducting due diligence.

Why is the PID preferred over a civic address when identifying a property in BC real estate transactions?

A civic address is assigned by a local municipality and can change due to renaming of streets, renumbering schemes, or amalgamation of jurisdictions, whereas a PID is a permanent identifier tied to the registered legal parcel in the BC land title system. Using the PID in a Contract of Purchase and Sale ensures that the exact parcel being transferred is unambiguously identified, reducing the risk of errors or disputes arising from address discrepancies. BCFSA guidance under RESA emphasizes accurate property identification as part of a licensee's professional obligations.

Does a strata lot in BC have its own PID?

Yes, each strata lot created under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) is registered as a separate parcel in the BC land title system and is therefore assigned its own unique PID. Common property and limited common property within a strata plan do not receive individual PIDs because they are not separately registered parcels available for individual sale. When conducting a title search or completing a strata property transaction, the strata lot's specific PID should be used alongside the strata plan number to accurately identify the unit.

Can a parcel in the Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) be identified by PID in BC?

Yes, parcels located within BC's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) are registered in the land title system and carry PIDs like any other parcel. The PID identifies the physical parcel but does not itself indicate ALR status; buyers must separately verify ALR inclusion through the Agricultural Land Commission (ALC) or the LTSA, as ALR designation affects permitted uses under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36). Confirming the correct PID is an essential first step before applying to the ALC for any non-farm use, subdivision, or exclusion application.

Is the PID used when filing a Property Transfer Tax return in BC?

Yes, the PID of the property being transferred must be included on the Property Transfer Tax (PTT) return submitted to the BC Ministry of Finance under the Property Transfer Tax Act. Accurate PID entry ensures that the correct parcel is recorded for tax purposes, including the application of PTT tiers (1% on the first portion of fair market value, 2% on the next portion, 3% on higher values, and an additional 2% on residential property value exceeding $3,000,000). Errors in PID entry on a PTT return may cause delays in registration at the LTSA and could require a corrected filing.

What happens to a PID when a parcel is subdivided in British Columbia?

When a parcel is subdivided, the original parcel's PID is retired and each newly created lot is assigned its own unique PID by the LTSA upon registration of the subdivision plan. The retired PID is not reassigned to any future parcel, ensuring the historical uniqueness of every identifier in the land title system. Developers and licensees should be aware that pre-sale contracts referencing a parent parcel's PID will need to be updated once the subdivision is registered and new PIDs are issued.

How does a PID relate to a legal description of property in BC?

A legal description — which typically includes the lot number, plan number, district lot, and land district — describes the parcel's boundaries within BC's surveying framework, while the PID is a numeric shorthand that uniquely indexes that legal description in the LTSA's registry. Both identifiers refer to the same parcel, but the PID provides a faster and error-resistant way to retrieve title records electronically. In BC real estate contracts, best practice is to include both the PID and the full legal description to ensure complete and accurate property identification.

Do BC real estate licensees have any obligations related to correctly identifying the PID in a transaction?

Yes, BCFSA-licensed real estate professionals operating under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and its Rules have a duty to act with reasonable care and skill, which includes ensuring that the property being bought or sold is accurately identified in all transaction documents. Using an incorrect PID in a Contract of Purchase and Sale could constitute a material error and may expose a licensee to regulatory action by the BCFSA. Licensees should always verify the PID through an LTSA title search rather than relying solely on listing data or the seller's representation.
