Strata Bylaws

Strata bylaws are the rules governing the internal operation of a strata corporation, enacted and enforced under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43 (SPA). They are subordinate to the SPA and cannot conflict with it. Bylaws commonly address matters such as pets, rentals, parking, move-in and move-out procedures, noise, renovation approvals, and appearance standards, though the specific content varies by strata corporation. Under the SPA, bylaws must be filed at the Land Title Office to take effect against owners and others. Because bylaws restricting pets or rentals can materially affect a property's suitability for a given buyer, reviewing the registered bylaws is a standard part of pre-completion due diligence. Verify current bylaw details and any recent SPA amendments with a BC lawyer or notary.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are strata bylaws in British Columbia?

Strata bylaws are the rules that govern the operation and use of a strata corporation's property and common property, created under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43. They are passed by a resolution of the strata corporation and registered at the Land Title Office. Bylaws must not conflict with the Strata Property Act or Strata Property Regulation; any bylaw provision that does conflict is unenforceable.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Can strata bylaws restrict or prohibit pet ownership?

Yes. Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, a strata corporation may pass bylaws restricting or prohibiting the keeping of pets, including rules about number, size, or breed. However, section 123 of the Act prohibits bylaws that prevent certain assistance or service animals for persons with disabilities, and section 144 allows a court to declare a bylaw significantly unfair. Buyers should review a strata's bylaws carefully if they own or plan to own a pet.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Can strata bylaws prohibit or restrict rentals?

Yes. The Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, permits strata corporations to pass bylaws that restrict or prohibit rentals, including short-term rentals, subject to certain limitations. For example, section 143 protects existing owners who rented before a rental restriction bylaw was passed (the 'rental restriction bylaw' provisions). Verify the exact terms of any rental restriction bylaw with a BC lawyer or notary, as these can significantly affect a buyer's ability to rent out their unit.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Where can I obtain a copy of a strata corporation's bylaws?

Strata bylaws are registered at the Land Title Office and can be obtained as part of a title search or strata document package. Under section 36 of the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, a buyer (or their representative) is entitled to request a copy of the bylaws and other strata records from the strata corporation. Most buyers' real estate licensees or lawyers will order a full strata information package, including bylaws, as part of their due diligence during the subject-removal period.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
How are strata bylaws created or amended?

Under sections 125–128 of the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, strata bylaws are created or amended by a resolution passed by a three-quarters vote at an annual or special general meeting. Bylaws take effect when filed at the Land Title Office. The strata council cannot amend bylaws; only the owners voting at a general meeting can do so.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
What happens if a strata bylaw conflicts with the Strata Property Act?

Any bylaw provision that conflicts with the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, or the Strata Property Regulation is unenforceable. Section 121 of the Act provides that bylaws must not contravene the Act, the regulations, or the Human Rights Code. If a buyer or owner believes a bylaw is invalid, significantly unfair, or in conflict with the Act, they should verify their rights with a BC lawyer or notary.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Can a strata corporation enforce its bylaws against an owner or tenant?

Yes. Under sections 129–135 of the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, a strata corporation may enforce bylaws by levying fines (after proper notice and hearing) or seeking a court order for compliance. Owners are responsible for ensuring that their tenants and guests also comply with the strata's bylaws and rules. Fines and enforcement procedures must follow the procedural fairness requirements set out in the Act and bylaws.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Do strata bylaws apply to tenants as well as owners?

Yes. Under section 26 of the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, tenants must comply with the Act, the regulations, the bylaws, and the rules of the strata corporation. Owners are responsible for any contravention of the bylaws by their tenants, occupants, or visitors. Landlords should provide tenants with a copy of current bylaws and rules to avoid enforcement issues.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
What is the difference between strata bylaws and strata rules?

Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, bylaws are passed by a three-quarters vote of owners at a general meeting and must be filed at the Land Title Office (sections 125–128). Rules are made by the strata council under bylaw authority, require only majority council approval, do not need to be registered, and typically address day-to-day operational matters (section 125). Both are enforceable, but bylaws take precedence over rules if there is a conflict.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
Why should a buyer review strata bylaws before completing a purchase?

Strata bylaws can materially affect a property's suitability and value by restricting pets, rentals (including short-term rentals), parking, renovations, and other uses. Under the Real Estate Services Act, SBC 2004, c. 42, and the RESA Rules, a buyer's representative must help the buyer understand material facts, and reviewing bylaws is a standard due-diligence step during the subject-removal period. Buyers should verify how specific bylaw provisions may affect their intended use of the property with a BC lawyer or notary before removing subjects.

Verify with: BC Government Strata Housing
