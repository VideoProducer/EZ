Building Permit

A building permit is an official authorization issued by a local government under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, permitting construction, alteration, or demolition of a building in accordance with applicable bylaws and the BC Building Code. The permit process confirms that proposed work meets safety and land-use standards before work begins. Most material structural or mechanical work requires a permit; however, the specific scope of what triggers a permit obligation varies by municipality — verify current requirements with your local authority. Unpermitted work may affect a property's resale value, insurability, and eligibility for mortgage financing; buyers and licensees should confirm permit status through the local authority having jurisdiction. Verify current details with a BC lawyer, notary, or licensed tax professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a building permit in British Columbia?

A building permit is an authorization issued by a local government (municipality, regional district, or Islands Trust area) allowing construction, alteration, or demolition of a building. Under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, local governments adopt bylaws requiring permits to ensure compliance with the BC Building Code and local zoning and safety standards. Most structural work, additions, new construction, and certain renovations require a permit before work begins. Verify the specific requirements with your local government building department before commencing any project.

Do I always need a building permit for renovation or construction work in BC?

Not all work requires a permit, but most structural changes, new construction, additions, certain re-roofing, plumbing, electrical, and HVAC installations do. Local government bylaws adopted under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, specify which projects require permits; exemptions vary by municipality and often cover minor repairs or cosmetic updates. Starting work without a required permit can result in stop-work orders, fines, mandatory removal of unpermitted work, and difficulties with insurance, resale, or financing. Verify the specific scope and permit requirements with your local government building department and a BC lawyer or notary before beginning any project.

What happens if I sell a property with unpermitted work in BC?

Unpermitted work must generally be disclosed to buyers under the common-law duty of disclosure and, for licensed real estate professionals, the Real Estate Services Act (RESA), SBC 2004, c. 42, and RESA Rules enforced by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA). Buyers may request permit records; if unpermitted work is discovered, they can negotiate price reductions, demand retroactive permits and inspections, or in some cases rescind the contract. Unpermitted work can also affect title insurance, homeowner's insurance, and mortgage financing. Verify disclosure obligations and remediation options with a BC lawyer or notary before listing a property with known unpermitted alterations.

Verify with: Building Permits
Can unpermitted building work affect my mortgage or home insurance in BC?

Yes. Lenders and insurers commonly require that all structural work be properly permitted and inspected; unpermitted additions, electrical, plumbing, or structural changes may void coverage or trigger mortgage covenant breaches. If a loss occurs in an area with unpermitted work, insurers may deny the claim or reduce the payout. Financial institutions regulated under federal law (Bank Act, SC 1991, c. 46) and provincial consumer protection frameworks expect properties to meet building code standards. Verify the status of all building permits and consult a BC lawyer, notary, and your lender or insurer before purchasing or renovating a property with unknown permit history.

How do I check if a building permit was issued for work done on a BC property?

Contact the local government building department (city, district, or regional district) where the property is located; most maintain public records of issued permits, inspections, and occupancy certificates. Some municipalities offer online permit search portals; others require an in-person or written request. Under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1, these records are generally public. If no permit is found for known construction, verify next steps—including the possibility of retroactive permits or professional engineer reports—with a BC lawyer or notary before completing a purchase or sale.

Verify with: Building Permits
What is the role of the BC Building Code in the building permit process?

The BC Building Code (adopted under provincial regulation) sets minimum safety, structural, fire, plumbing, and energy-efficiency standards for buildings; local governments enforce the Code through their permit and inspection processes under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. A building permit application is reviewed to confirm the proposed work complies with the current Code and local zoning bylaws. Once approved, inspections at key stages (foundation, framing, final) verify compliance before occupancy is granted. Verify Code requirements and inspection schedules with your local building department and design professionals (architect, engineer) before starting construction.

Verify with: Building Permits
Can I apply for a building permit retroactively if work was done without one in BC?

Many local governments allow retroactive permit applications, but approval is not guaranteed and may require opening walls, engineering reports, or bringing older work up to current BC Building Code standards, which can be costly. The local government may levy fines and require full inspections under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. Retroactive permits do not erase potential liability for past violations or discharge disclosure duties on resale. Verify the feasibility, cost, and legal implications of retroactive permitting with your local building department, a BC lawyer, and a licensed engineer or architect before proceeding.

Verify with: Building Permits
Who can apply for a building permit in BC—owner or contractor?

Either the property owner or a licensed contractor (with the owner's authorization) may apply for a building permit, depending on local government bylaw requirements under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. Many municipalities require that certain trades (electrical, plumbing, gas) be performed by provincially licensed tradespeople, and permit applications may need to specify the licensee. Owner-builders performing their own work may face additional requirements, insurance obligations, or bonding. Verify application rules, required licenses, and liability with your local building department and a BC lawyer or notary before submitting a permit application.

Verify with: Building Permits
How long is a building permit valid in BC, and what happens if work is not completed in time?

Permit validity periods are set by each local government's bylaws under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1; many municipalities issue permits valid for 12 to 24 months (as of 2026-07-27 — verify current) from the date of issuance, with extensions available upon request and payment of fees. If work is not completed or inspections not passed before expiry, the permit may lapse, requiring a new application, updated plans, and compliance with any intervening Code or bylaw changes. Verify your permit's expiry date and extension procedures with your local building department immediately if construction is delayed.

Verify with: Building Permits
What are the consequences of ignoring a stop-work order on unpermitted construction in BC?

Local governments enforce building permit requirements through stop-work orders and, if ignored, fines, prosecution, and court orders requiring removal or correction of the work under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. Continued non-compliance can result in daily fines, liens on the property, or injunctions. Unpermitted work may also void insurance, create title defects, and trigger disclosure obligations that complicate or block resale. Verify your legal obligations and remediation options immediately with a BC lawyer if you receive a stop-work order or discover unpermitted work on your property.
