Rainscreen

A rainscreen is a building envelope system that incorporates a drainage cavity between the exterior cladding and the wall assembly, allowing moisture to drain and air to circulate rather than accumulate within the wall. Following widespread building envelope failures in British Columbia during the 1990s — commonly called the "leaky condo crisis" — provincial building code requirements evolved to address rainscreen construction standards; verify current requirements with a BC lawyer or licensed building professional. Buildings constructed before mandatory rainscreen provisions were in force may lack this drainage cavity, making them a higher-risk category for water intrusion and envelope damage. Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, strata corporations are required to maintain common property, which typically includes the building envelope; depreciation reports may document envelope condition and repair history. Buyers conducting due diligence on older strata properties in coastal BC commonly review construction year, building envelope inspection reports, and rainscreen status before completing a purchase.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a rainscreen in BC real estate?

A rainscreen is a building envelope design that includes an air gap between the exterior cladding and the wall sheathing, allowing moisture to drain and ventilate. This design prevents water intrusion and rot by creating a capillary break and ventilation path. Rainscreen construction became standard practice in coastal BC following the leaky condo crisis of the 1990s. Buildings constructed before 2000 may lack proper rainscreen systems — a major risk factor for water intrusion.

Verify with: BC Building Code
Are rainscreens required by law in British Columbia?

Rainscreen requirements are primarily governed by the BC Building Code, which is adopted and enforced at the municipal level under the Local Government Act, RSBC 2015, c. 1. Municipalities enforce building standards through their building inspection and permitting processes. The BC Building Code incorporated rainscreen design principles for exposed residential construction in coastal climates following the leaky condo crisis, but specific standards evolved over time (as of 2026-07-27 — verify current building code requirements with your municipality).

Verify with: BC Building Code
Why should I care about rainscreen when buying a pre-2000 condo in BC?

Buildings constructed before 2000 may lack proper rainscreen systems and are at higher risk for water intrusion, rot, and mould — issues that triggered the BC leaky condo crisis. Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, the strata corporation is responsible for maintaining and repairing common property, including building envelopes, which can result in costly special levies for owners. Buyers should review depreciation reports (required under s. 94 of the Strata Property Act for stratas with five or more units, as of 2026-07-27 — verify current), building envelope reports, and Form B (Information Certificate) for any history of envelope repairs or litigation before purchasing.

Verify with: BC Building Code
What is a building envelope report and how does it relate to rainscreen?

A building envelope report is an engineering assessment of a building's exterior walls, roof, windows, and waterproofing systems, including whether a rainscreen design is present and functioning properly. These reports are not mandated by the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, but are commonly commissioned by strata corporations as part of depreciation report preparation or when water intrusion is suspected. Buyers should request any available building envelope reports during the due diligence period and verify current building condition with a BC lawyer or building inspector before completing a purchase.

Verify with: BC Building Code
Does the Property Disclosure Statement require sellers to disclose rainscreen deficiencies?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA), SBC 2004, c. 42, and RESA Rules, BC does not mandate a universal seller property disclosure statement for all transactions; disclosure obligations are governed by common law and contract terms. However, sellers and their licensees have a duty not to misrepresent material latent defects. If a seller is aware of water intrusion, lack of rainscreen, or past envelope repairs, failure to disclose may constitute misrepresentation. Buyers should ask specific questions, review strata documents under the Strata Property Act (especially the Form B and depreciation report), and verify current building condition with a BC lawyer or building inspector.

Verify with: BC Building Code
Can I get a home inspection to check for rainscreen issues?

Yes. Home inspections are a standard due-diligence step in BC real estate transactions and are typically included as a subject condition in the Contract of Purchase and Sale. A qualified home inspector or building envelope specialist can assess whether a rainscreen system is present, properly installed, and functioning, and may identify signs of water intrusion such as rot, mould, or staining. Verify the inspector's qualifications and scope of work, and consult a BC lawyer if the inspection reveals material defects before waiving your subject conditions.

Verify with: BC Building Code
What is a depreciation report and does it cover rainscreen status?

A depreciation report is a long-term capital planning document required under s. 94 of the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, for strata corporations with five or more strata lots (as of 2026-07-27 — verify current). The report must include an inventory of common property and common assets, their condition and estimated remaining useful life, and a funding plan for repair and replacement. A properly prepared depreciation report should address the building envelope, including rainscreen systems if present, and forecast any necessary repairs. Buyers should review the depreciation report and Form B (Information Certificate) to understand upcoming special levies or maintenance obligations before purchasing a strata unit.

Verify with: BC Building Code
If my strata building needs rainscreen retrofitting, who pays?

Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, the strata corporation is responsible for maintaining and repairing common property, including the building envelope (ss. 72 and 73, as of 2026-07-27 — verify current). The cost is typically funded through a special levy approved by a 3/4 vote at a general meeting (s. 108) or, if planned, through contributions to the contingency reserve fund. Individual owners pay their proportionate share of special levies based on their unit entitlement. Verify the strata's financial health, depreciation report, and any planned envelope work with a BC lawyer or strata management professional before purchasing.

Verify with: BC Building Code
Are there any BC government programs to help with rainscreen repairs?

Historically, the provincial government established the Barrett Commission and later offered limited assistance programs following the 1990s leaky condo crisis, but most relief programs have since closed (as of 2026-07-27 — verify current). Strata corporations and owners should consult the BC Ministry of Housing and the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) websites for any current grant or loan programs. Verify eligibility and application details with a BC lawyer, strata management professional, or the relevant ministry before proceeding.

Verify with: BC Building Code
Does the absence of a rainscreen affect my property's resale value or insurability?

Buildings without proper rainscreen systems or with a history of water intrusion may face lower market values, reduced buyer interest, and difficulty obtaining strata property insurance due to higher risk of future claims. Under the Strata Property Act, SBC 1998, c. 43, the strata corporation must obtain and maintain adequate insurance for common property (s. 149, as of 2026-07-27 — verify current), but insurers may charge higher premiums or exclude coverage for pre-existing envelope defects. Buyers should review the strata's insurance policy, claims history (available in the Form B), and any engineering reports, and verify current market conditions and insurability with a BC lawyer, insurance broker, or REALTOR® before purchasing.

Verify with: BC Building Code
