Equity Takeout

An equity takeout is borrowing additional funds against the equity in a home, typically through a refinance, second mortgage, or HELOC. In Canada borrowers can usually access up to 80% of the home's appraised value (less the existing mortgage). Often used for renovations, debt consolidation, investing, or a down payment on another property.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an equity takeout and how does it work in British Columbia?

An equity takeout is the process of borrowing against the accumulated equity in a BC property, typically through a refinance, second mortgage, or Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC). In Canada, federally regulated lenders generally allow borrowers to access up to 80% of the property's appraised value, minus any outstanding mortgage balance. The funds can be used for purposes such as renovations, debt consolidation, or a down payment on a second property. The specific product and terms available will depend on the lender and the borrower's financial profile.

Does taking equity out of a BC home trigger Property Transfer Tax?

No, an equity takeout — whether through a refinance, HELOC, or second mortgage — does not constitute a transfer of a registered interest in the property that triggers Property Transfer Tax under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act. Property Transfer Tax is levied on taxable transactions involving the transfer of a beneficial or legal interest in land, which a refinancing or secured borrowing arrangement does not constitute. Borrowers should nonetheless confirm their specific transaction structure with a BC notary or lawyer to ensure no registrable transfer is occurring.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
Can a BC homeowner use an equity takeout to fund a down payment on a second property, and are there any strata-related considerations?

Yes, BC homeowners commonly use equity takeout proceeds as a down payment on a second property, including a strata lot. If the property being purchased is a strata lot, the buyer should review the Form B Information Certificate issued by the strata corporation under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) to understand any outstanding strata fees, special levies, or litigation that could affect the investment's viability. The strata corporation's financial health, including its contingency reserve fund balance disclosed in the Form B, is an important consideration when using leveraged funds for a strata purchase.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
Does a BC real estate licensee have any disclosure obligations when helping a client who is using equity takeout funds to purchase another property?

Yes. Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules administered by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), a licensee must act in the best interests of their client and disclose all known material information relevant to the transaction. While a licensee is not required to advise on the wisdom of the financing strategy, they must not misrepresent how the funds are being sourced and must disclose any conflict of interest — for example, if the licensee or a related party has a financial interest in either property involved. Licensees should direct clients to mortgage brokers or lawyers for advice on the borrowing arrangements themselves.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
How does an equity takeout affect a homeowner's position if their BC property goes into foreclosure?

In British Columbia, foreclosure is a judicial process governed by the BC Supreme Court Civil Rules and the Law and Equity Act; there is no power-of-sale mechanism as exists in some other provinces. If a borrower defaults on a loan secured by an equity takeout — such as a second mortgage or HELOC — the lender may commence foreclosure proceedings, and priority among secured creditors is generally determined by the order of registration of charges in the BC Land Title Office. A second mortgage lender therefore ranks behind the first mortgage holder, meaning they may recover less or nothing if the property's sale proceeds are insufficient to satisfy all secured debts.

Can equity be taken out of a property located in British Columbia's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR)?

A property situated within the Agricultural Land Reserve can be refinanced or have equity accessed in the same way as any other property, as the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36) and the ALR use regulations do not restrict a landowner's ability to borrow against the land. However, potential lenders and buyers should be aware that ALR designation restricts non-farm use and subdivision, which can affect the property's appraised value and marketability — factors that lenders consider when determining how much equity is accessible. Landowners with ALR properties should contact the Agricultural Land Commission directly regarding any proposed use changes that might affect the property's value or intended purpose.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
Are there privacy obligations a BC real estate licensee must follow when a client discloses they are completing an equity takeout?

Yes. Under BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), a licensee who collects personal or financial information from a client — including the fact that the client is completing an equity takeout — must use that information only for the purposes for which it was collected, obtain appropriate consent, and keep it secure. A licensee cannot share a client's financial details with third parties without authorization, and must not use that information for marketing purposes beyond what PIPA and, where applicable, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) permit. Clients should be informed of how their personal information will be used, stored, and protected.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
If a BC homeowner dies while having an outstanding equity takeout (e.g., a HELOC or second mortgage), how is it handled in the estate?

Under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA) of BC, a deceased person's secured debts — including a HELOC or second mortgage registered against their property — form part of the liabilities of the estate and must generally be addressed before assets are distributed to beneficiaries. The executor or administrator of the estate is responsible for managing the property, and the secured lender retains their registered charge on the land title, which must be discharged, assumed, or otherwise resolved as part of the estate administration. Beneficiaries who inherit the property may need to refinance or sell to satisfy the outstanding secured obligation, depending on the terms of the loan.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
Does completing an equity takeout on a BC strata property require strata corporation approval?

No, a strata lot owner does not require approval from the strata corporation under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) simply to refinance their unit or obtain a HELOC or second mortgage, as these are private financial arrangements registered against the owner's individual strata lot title. However, a lender may require an updated Form B Information Certificate from the strata corporation to confirm there are no outstanding special levies, arrears, or significant liabilities that could affect the security of the loan. Strata bylaws may be relevant if any intended use of the equity takeout funds — such as renovation work — requires strata council approval under the corporation's bylaws.

Verify with: Financial Consumer Agency of Canada
Can a BC real estate licensee receive a referral fee from a mortgage broker for referring a client seeking an equity takeout?

The receipt of referral fees by a BC real estate licensee is governed by the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules established by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), which require that any remuneration — including referral fees from third parties such as mortgage brokers — be disclosed to the client. A licensee must not accept undisclosed referral fees, as this can constitute a breach of their duty of disclosure and could undermine the client's trust. Licensees should consult current BCFSA guidance and their managing broker to ensure any referral arrangement is structured and disclosed in compliance with RESA and its Rules.
