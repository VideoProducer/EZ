Principal Residence Exemption

A federal tax provision under Income Tax Act s. 40(2)(b) and s. 54 that may exempt all or part of the capital gain on the disposition of a property that qualified as the taxpayer's principal residence for each year of ownership. Designation is made on Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND) at the time of filing. The exemption uses a "one-plus" formula: (years designated as principal residence + 1) ÷ years owned, multiplied by the capital gain. Only one property per family unit (taxpayer, spouse/common-law partner, and unmarried minor children) may be designated as principal residence for any given year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Principal Residence Exemption and does it apply to BC homeowners?

The Principal Residence Exemption is a federal provision under Income Tax Act sections 40(2)(b) and 54 that can eliminate all or part of the capital gain realized when a taxpayer disposes of a property that qualified as their principal residence. It applies equally to BC homeowners as it does to all Canadian taxpayers, because it is a federal income tax rule rather than a BC-specific statute. The exemption is claimed by filing Schedule 3 and Form T2091(IND) with the Canada Revenue Agency in the tax year the disposition occurs.

Does claiming the Principal Residence Exemption in BC also eliminate Property Transfer Tax obligations on a future purchase?

No — the Principal Residence Exemption is a federal income tax concept and has no bearing on BC's Property Transfer Tax, which is governed by the BC Property Transfer Tax Act and is triggered on the acquisition of property, not on the sale. PTT is calculated at 1% on the first $200,000 of fair market value, 2% on the portion between $200,000 and $3,000,000, and 3% on the residential portion above $3,000,000, regardless of whether the seller claimed any capital gains exemption on a prior property. Buyers seeking PTT relief must qualify separately under programs such as the First-Time Home Buyers' Program or the Newly Built Home Exemption under the Property Transfer Tax Act.

Can a BC strata lot (condominium unit) qualify as a principal residence for the purposes of this exemption?

Yes — a strata lot in BC can qualify as a principal residence provided the taxpayer ordinarily inhabits it as their housing unit during the years for which the designation is made, meeting the requirements of Income Tax Act section 54. The fact that the property is governed by the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) and held by a strata corporation as common property structure does not disqualify the individually titled strata lot from being designated. Owners should ensure they hold registered title to the strata lot, as the exemption applies to the disposition of the taxpayer's interest in real property.

How does the 'one-plus' formula work when a BC homeowner has not designated the property as their principal residence for every year they owned it?

The formula under Income Tax Act section 40(2)(b) calculates the exempt portion of the capital gain as: (number of years designated as principal residence + 1) ÷ total number of years owned, multiplied by the capital gain. The extra '1' in the numerator is a Canada-wide rule designed to bridge gaps when a taxpayer moves between properties in the same calendar year. For example, a BC homeowner who owned a property for 10 years but designated it for only 7 years would have an exempt fraction of 8/10 of the gain, with the remaining 2/10 being taxable.

Does the BC Speculation and Vacancy Tax affect a property owner's ability to claim the Principal Residence Exemption?

The BC Speculation and Vacancy Tax and the federal Principal Residence Exemption are separate and independent regimes: the Speculation and Vacancy Tax is an annual provincial tax administered under BC legislation targeting properties not used as a primary residence in designated BC regions, while the Principal Residence Exemption is a federal income tax provision applied at the time of disposition. Qualifying for one does not automatically qualify a taxpayer for the other, as each has its own definitions, tests, and filing requirements. Owners in designated Speculation and Vacancy Tax areas should consult current BC Ministry of Finance guidance to understand their annual obligations independently of any capital gains designation.

If a BC homeowner rents out part of their home, can they still claim the full Principal Residence Exemption?

Partial rental use of a principal residence can affect the availability of the full exemption under the Income Tax Act, particularly if the owner has claimed capital cost allowance on the rental portion or if the Canada Revenue Agency determines that a change in use has occurred. BC-specific rules do not modify this federal analysis, but BC licensees governed by the Real Estate Services Act and the BCFSA should be aware that advising on the tax consequences of rental arrangements falls outside the scope of real estate services and requires a qualified tax professional. The CRA's administrative position on 'incidental' rental use without a structural change in use is set out in its interpretation bulletins, which taxpayers should review with a tax adviser.

Can a BC resident designate a vacation or recreational property (such as a cabin at a lake) as their principal residence?

A vacation or recreational property in BC can qualify as a principal residence under Income Tax Act section 54 for years in which the taxpayer or a qualifying family member 'ordinarily inhabited' it as their housing unit, even if it was not their primary home for the full year. However, only one property per family unit — comprising the taxpayer, their spouse or common-law partner, and unmarried minor children — may be designated for any given calendar year, so designating the cabin means the primary home cannot also be designated for that same year. The decision of which property to designate for which years is a tax planning matter that requires professional advice, not a real estate services function under RESA.

What reporting obligations does a BC taxpayer have when selling a principal residence, and are there penalties for failing to report?

Since the 2016 tax year, the Canada Revenue Agency requires all taxpayers who dispose of a principal residence to report the disposition on Schedule 3 and file Form T2091(IND) in the year of sale, even if the full gain is exempt. Failure to report can result in the CRA denying the exemption entirely or assessing a late-designation penalty. This is a federal income tax obligation and is not administered by BC provincial authorities or the BCFSA under the Real Estate Services Act; however, BC licensees should encourage clients to obtain tax advice before completing a disposition.

Does farmland in BC's Agricultural Land Reserve qualify for the Principal Residence Exemption on the residence portion?

A residence situated on Agricultural Land Reserve farmland in BC may qualify for the Principal Residence Exemption on the portion of the gain attributable to the housing unit and the surrounding land that reasonably contributes to its use as a residence, under the Income Tax Act definition in section 54 — but the farm land itself is generally subject to separate capital gains treatment, potentially including the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption for qualified farm property. The ALR is governed by the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), and any subdivision or disposition of ALR land must comply with ALC requirements, which operate independently of the federal tax exemption. Owners of ALR properties should seek advice from both a tax professional and the Agricultural Land Commission regarding their specific circumstances.

When a BC property owner dies, can their estate claim the Principal Residence Exemption on a property sold or deemed disposed by the estate?

Under the Income Tax Act, a deemed disposition occurs at death at fair market value, and the deceased's legal representative may be able to designate the property as the deceased's principal residence for eligible years up to and including the year of death, potentially sheltering the gain. In BC, the administration of a deceased's estate — including filing the terminal tax return and claiming available exemptions — is governed by executor duties under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (SBC 2009, c. 13) (WESA), and the executor or administrator bears responsibility for ensuring the correct CRA filings are made. Because this involves both federal tax law and BC estate administration law, the estate should engage both a tax professional and a BC estates lawyer.
