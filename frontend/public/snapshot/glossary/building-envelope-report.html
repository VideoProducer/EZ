Building Envelope Report

A building envelope report is a specific engineering assessment of the condition of a building's exterior shell — the roof, windows, exterior walls, and waterproofing membranes — that keeps weather out. The leaky condo crisis of the 1980s and 1990s in BC was caused by building envelope failures. Condos and townhouses built between 1982 and 2000 are commonly assessed by a current building envelope report. A full envelope remediation can cost $50,000 to $150,000 per unit. Request this report for any building in that era before making an offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a building envelope report and why is it particularly important for BC condos built between 1982 and 2000?

A building envelope report is an engineering assessment of a building's exterior shell — including the roof, exterior walls, windows, and waterproofing membranes — evaluating how well it resists moisture intrusion. Buildings constructed in BC between roughly 1982 and 2000 are associated with the leaky condo crisis, during which widespread design and construction deficiencies caused significant water ingress and structural damage. A current building envelope report for a strata lot in that era can reveal whether remediation has been completed, is ongoing, or is still needed. Prospective buyers of strata lots in buildings of that vintage commonly request this report before making an offer.

Is a strata corporation in BC legally required to obtain or maintain a building envelope report?

The Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) does not explicitly mandate that every strata corporation obtain a building envelope report as a standalone document. However, the Act requires strata corporations to repair and maintain common property and common assets, which includes the building envelope, and to obtain a depreciation report (unless the strata corporation has validly waived that requirement by a three-quarters vote each year). A depreciation report prepared under the Strata Property Act Regulation must address the condition and estimated renewal costs of major common property components, which typically includes envelope elements such as roofing, cladding, and windows. Where envelope concerns exist, a dedicated engineering report may be necessary to fulfil the strata corporation's maintenance and disclosure obligations.

Where would a buyer find a building envelope report for a BC strata property they are considering purchasing?

Under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43), a strata corporation must provide certain records and documents upon request, including engineering reports, council meeting minutes, and the depreciation report. A prospective buyer or their licensee can request a Form B Information Certificate from the strata corporation, which triggers the strata corporation's obligation to disclose certain financial and legal information, and can also separately request copies of any building envelope or other engineering reports held by the strata corporation. Buyers commonly obtain these documents through their real estate licensee as part of the subject-removal due-diligence process. If the strata corporation does not hold a current report, a buyer may arrange an independent building envelope inspection as a subject condition in their offer.

What is the typical cost range for a full building envelope remediation in BC, and how does it affect strata owners?

A full building envelope remediation in BC can cost approximately $50,000 to $150,000 per strata unit, depending on building size, extent of damage, and materials required, though actual costs vary considerably and owners should obtain current engineering and contractor estimates. These costs may be funded through the strata corporation's contingency reserve fund, special levies approved by the owners, or a combination of both, under the framework of the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43). If the contingency reserve fund is insufficient, the strata corporation can pass a special levy requiring individual owners to contribute their proportionate share. Prospective buyers should review the strata corporation's financial statements, Form B, and any outstanding special levy resolutions to assess their potential financial exposure.

Does a BC real estate licensee have a duty to disclose a known building envelope problem to a buyer?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules established by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), a licensee acting for a buyer has a duty to act in the client's best interests and to disclose all known material information relevant to the transaction. A known or suspected building envelope deficiency is material information that a licensee must disclose to their buyer client. Licensees must not misrepresent or conceal material latent defects, and a significant building envelope problem — particularly one affecting habitability or requiring substantial cost to repair — would generally be considered a material latent defect. Licensees should also advise buyer clients to review all available engineering reports and obtain independent professional assessments where warranted.

How does a building envelope report relate to the depreciation report required under the BC Strata Property Act?

Under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) and its Regulation, a depreciation report must include an inventory of a strata corporation's common property and common assets, an assessment of their condition, and a financial plan projecting repair and replacement costs over a 30-year period. The building envelope — comprising roofing, exterior cladding, windows, and waterproofing — is typically among the highest-value components covered in a depreciation report. A standalone building envelope report prepared by a professional engineer provides a more detailed technical analysis than a depreciation report alone and may be referenced within or commissioned alongside a depreciation report. Where a building envelope report identifies significant deficiencies, the depreciation report's financial projections should reflect the anticipated remediation costs.

Can a buyer in BC make an offer on a leaky-condo-era strata lot conditional on review of a building envelope report?

Yes, a buyer can include a subject condition in their offer to purchase that makes the contract conditional upon the buyer's satisfactory review of a current building envelope report, along with other strata documents. Subject conditions are a standard and widely used mechanism in BC residential real estate transactions, giving the buyer a defined period to conduct due diligence before the contract becomes unconditional. A real estate licensee acting for the buyer has a duty under RESA and BCFSA standards to advise the client on the importance of appropriate subject conditions, particularly for strata lots in buildings with known or suspected envelope vulnerabilities. If no current building envelope report exists, the subject condition can be structured to allow the buyer to commission an independent envelope inspection.

Does an unresolved building envelope issue affect the Property Transfer Tax payable when buying a BC strata lot?

Property Transfer Tax (PTT) under BC's Property Transfer Tax Act is calculated on the fair market value of the property at the time of transfer, using tiered rates of 1% on the first $200,000, 2% on the portion from $200,001 to $3,000,000, 3% on the portion above $3,000,000, and an additional 2% on the residential portion above $3,000,000. An unresolved building envelope issue may affect the fair market value of the property, and therefore the PTT base, but the tax itself is assessed on whatever fair market value is established at transfer — the existence of a deficiency does not create a separate PTT exemption or reduction. Buyers should consult the BC Ministry of Finance guidance and a qualified professional regarding how a known deficiency might be reflected in the agreed purchase price and corresponding PTT calculation. First-time buyers and purchasers of newly built homes should separately consult current BCFSA and Ministry of Finance guidance on any applicable exemptions.

What professional qualifications should the author of a building envelope report hold in British Columbia?

In British Columbia, a building envelope report is typically prepared by a professional engineer (P.Eng.) or a registered architect (R.A.) with specific expertise in building science and building envelope assessment, regulated under the Engineers and Geoscientists Act and the Architects Act respectively. Some reports are also prepared by building envelope consultants who hold the Registered Envelope Professional (REP) designation, which is administered by RDH Building Science and recognized in BC industry practice, though this designation does not replace professional engineering or architectural registration where that is required by law. Buyers and strata corporations should confirm the qualifications and professional liability coverage of any consultant they engage. For significant remediation decisions, strata corporations typically require a report sealed by a professional engineer.

If a strata corporation has already completed building envelope remediation, how can a buyer confirm this and what documents should they request?

A buyer can confirm completed remediation by requesting copies of the original building envelope report, any subsequent engineering reports confirming completion of the work, strata council meeting minutes referencing the remediation project, and financial records showing how the remediation was funded under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) framework. The Form B Information Certificate issued by the strata corporation should disclose any unresolved special levies or legal proceedings related to the envelope, and the strata corporation's financial statements should reflect whether any remediation-related debt or special levy remains outstanding. Buyers may also request a statutory warranty or engineer's certificate confirming the scope and completion of the remediation work. Reviewing these documents with a qualified professional during the subject-removal period is advisable for leaky-condo-era buildings.
