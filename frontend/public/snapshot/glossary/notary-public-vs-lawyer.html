Notary Public vs Lawyer

In BC, either a notary public or a real estate lawyer can handle the legal conveyancing (transfer of property). Notaries typically charge less and handle straightforward transactions. Lawyers can handle more complex situations including disputes, foreclosures, and estate sales. Most residential purchases in BC are handled by notary publics. Fees typically range from $1,200-$2,000 plus disbursements.

Frequently Asked Questions

In British Columbia, can a notary public legally handle the conveyancing for a standard residential property purchase?

Yes. In BC, a notary public commissioned under the Notaries Act (RSBC 1996, c. 334) is fully authorized to handle the legal conveyancing for most straightforward residential property transfers, including preparing and registering the transfer documentation at the Land Title Office. They can also prepare and file the Property Transfer Tax return required under the Property Transfer Tax Act. For the majority of standard residential purchases in BC, a notary public provides all the legal conveyancing services needed.

What types of real estate transactions in BC are typically beyond the scope of a notary public and require a lawyer?

BC notaries are limited by the Notaries Act to non-contentious matters, meaning they cannot represent clients in disputes, litigation, or contested legal proceedings. Transactions involving foreclosure actions governed by the BC Supreme Court Civil Rules and the Law and Equity Act, complex estate sales requiring probate under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA), or title disputes generally require a lawyer. If a conveyance has an unusual or contentious element, a real estate lawyer is the appropriate professional to engage.

How much should I expect to pay for conveyancing by a notary public or lawyer in BC?

Professional fees for conveyancing in BC typically range from approximately $1,200 to $2,000 plus disbursements, with notary publics generally at the lower end of that range and lawyers sometimes charging more for complex files. Disbursements are separate out-of-pocket costs and include Land Title Office registration fees, title search fees, and other third-party charges. Fee amounts are not regulated at a fixed rate, so it is advisable to request a detailed written quote from your chosen professional before proceeding.

Is there a difference between a BC notary public and a lawyer when it comes to filing the Property Transfer Tax return?

No meaningful difference exists for standard filings — both notary publics and lawyers are authorized to prepare and submit Property Transfer Tax returns on behalf of purchasers under the Property Transfer Tax Act. They can also assess and document eligibility for exemptions such as the First-Time Home Buyers' exemption (full exemption on fair market value up to $835,000) or the Newly Built Home exemption (up to $1,100,000). For complex eligibility questions, your conveyancing professional may refer you to the BC Ministry of Finance.

Can a notary public in BC handle the purchase of a strata lot, including reviewing strata documents?

Yes, a notary public can handle the conveyancing for a strata lot purchase in BC, including registering the transfer under the Land Title Act and preparing the Property Transfer Tax return. However, reviewing and advising on strata documents — such as the Form B Information Certificate, Form F Certificate of Payment, depreciation reports, and bylaws under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) — falls within the buyer's due diligence, often conducted before the notary or lawyer becomes involved. If the strata documents reveal complex legal issues or disputes within the strata corporation, a lawyer may be better positioned to advise.

In a BC foreclosure purchase, do I need a lawyer rather than a notary public?

Yes. Foreclosure in BC is a judicial process governed by the BC Supreme Court Civil Rules and the Law and Equity Act — there is no power-of-sale mechanism as exists in some other provinces. Because a court order approving the sale (a Court Order Absolute or Order Nisi) is involved, the conveyancing and title issues arising from foreclosure sales are considered contentious matters, which fall outside the authorized scope of a BC notary public under the Notaries Act. A practicing BC lawyer is required to act on a foreclosure purchase.

If I am purchasing a BC property as part of an estate sale where the deceased's estate requires probate, do I need a lawyer?

Where the estate of a deceased owner has not yet received a grant of probate, the administration of the estate is governed by the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA), and obtaining probate from the BC Supreme Court is a legal proceeding that only a lawyer can conduct on behalf of the estate. A notary public is restricted to non-contentious matters under the Notaries Act and cannot provide representation in court proceedings. Once probate has been granted and the executor has clear authority to transfer title, a notary public may be able to handle the resulting conveyance if it is otherwise straightforward.

Do BC notary publics and lawyers both hold client funds in trust during a real estate transaction?

Yes. Both BC notary publics and real estate lawyers are required to hold client conveyancing funds — such as deposits and completion funds — in designated trust accounts governed by their respective professional bodies. Lawyers in BC are regulated by the Law Society of BC under the Legal Profession Act, and notaries are regulated under the Notaries Act, with both regimes imposing strict trust accounting obligations. Neither profession is regulated by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) for conveyancing trust accounts — BCFSA and the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) govern real estate licensees, not conveyancing professionals.

Can one notary public or lawyer represent both the buyer and the seller in the same BC property transaction?

Joint representation of buyer and seller by a single conveyancing professional in the same transaction is a well-recognized conflict-of-interest risk in BC. Both the Law Society of BC (for lawyers) and the Society of Notaries Public of BC (for notaries) have professional conduct rules that significantly restrict or prohibit acting for both parties when their interests conflict. In practice, buyers and sellers are strongly encouraged to retain separate conveyancing professionals to protect each party's independent legal interests throughout the transaction.

Does it matter whether I choose a notary public or a lawyer when it comes to registering my title at the BC Land Title Office?

No — both notary publics and lawyers in BC are authorized to prepare and submit land title documents electronically through the Land Title and Survey Authority (LTSA) using the myLTSA platform, and both can register transfers, mortgages, and other instruments on your behalf. The legal effect of registration, and the protections afforded by the Torrens title system under the Land Title Act, are identical regardless of which type of conveyancing professional you use. The choice between a notary and a lawyer is therefore driven by transaction complexity and personal preference rather than any difference in registration authority.
