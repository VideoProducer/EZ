Equestrian Property / Horse Property

An equestrian property (also called a horse property) is a residential property designed or used for horse care, training, and riding, typically including facilities such as a barn, stalls, paddocks, arenas, and pasture. These properties are often found in rural or agricultural areas and may be subject to Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) rules, municipal zoning for accessory buildings, manure-management requirements, and water-supply capacity for livestock. Confirming permitted equine use, fencing, and any environmental compliance obligations is a standard part of due diligence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an equestrian property in BC's Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) allowed to operate a commercial riding stable or training facility?

Under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), horse keeping and equestrian activities are generally considered farm use and are permitted within the ALR without an application, but operating a commercial stable or training facility that goes beyond farm use may require a non-farm-use application to the Agricultural Land Commission (ALC). The ALC determines whether specific activities qualify as farm use on a case-by-case basis, and local government zoning must also permit the proposed use. Buyers should contact the ALC and their local municipality or regional district directly to confirm what approvals, if any, are needed for their intended equestrian operation.

What due diligence should a BC buyer conduct regarding zoning and permitted use before purchasing a horse property?

A buyer should confirm with the relevant local government (municipality or regional district) that the zoning of the parcel expressly permits equine use, accessory farm buildings such as barns and arenas, and any ancillary residential use. If the property is within the ALR, the buyer should also verify with the ALC that the proposed equestrian activities qualify as permitted farm use under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36). A licensee acting for the buyer has a duty under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA rules to disclose known material information, but the buyer should independently verify zoning by obtaining written confirmation from the local authority.

How does being inside the Agricultural Land Reserve affect the subdivision potential of an equestrian property in BC?

Land within the ALR is subject to strict subdivision restrictions under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36), and subdivision generally requires ALC approval unless it meets specific exemption criteria set out in ALC regulations. Minimum lot sizes within the ALR are governed by both ALC rules and local government bylaws, and the ALC may refuse subdivision applications that it determines would impair the agricultural viability of the land. Buyers interested in subdividing an equestrian property that is wholly or partly within the ALR should consult the ALC directly for current subdivision requirements and parcel size thresholds.

What environmental and manure-management obligations might apply to a horse property owner in BC?

Horse property owners in BC may be subject to the Environmental Management Act (SBC 2003, c. 53) and associated regulations, including the BC Organic Matter Recycling Regulation and the Agricultural Waste Control Regulation, which set requirements for managing and storing manure to prevent contamination of water sources. Local governments may also impose additional conditions through zoning bylaws or development permits related to setbacks from watercourses and waste management practices. Buyers should request copies of any existing environmental compliance orders or operational certificates registered against the property and obtain independent advice on current provincial and local requirements.

Does Property Transfer Tax (PTT) apply differently to a BC equestrian property that is classified as a farm?

Property Transfer Tax under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act applies to all registrable transfers of land in BC, including equestrian and farm properties, based on the fair market value of the property at the time of transfer. The standard PTT rates are 1% on the first $200,000 of fair market value, 2% on the portion from $200,001 to $3,000,000, and 3% on the portion above $3,000,000. There is no blanket PTT exemption for farm-classified land, but buyers should confirm with the BC Ministry of Finance and a tax professional whether any specific exemption or reduced assessed value applicable to farm land could affect the PTT calculation in their circumstances.

What water-supply considerations are important when buying a horse property in BC?

Horses require a significant daily water supply, so buyers should investigate the source, capacity, and legal status of the water supply — whether it is a drilled well, surface licence, or connection to a water utility — before completing a purchase. In BC, water use rights for purposes beyond domestic use are governed by the Water Sustainability Act (SBC 2014, c. 15), and a separate water licence may be required for livestock watering beyond what is permitted under a domestic purpose licence. Buyers should request copies of any existing water licence certificates, well records filed with the BC Ministry of Water, Land and Resource Stewardship, and water quality test results as part of their due diligence.

How should a BC real estate licensee disclose known material latent defects on a horse property, such as unstable paddock soil or a contaminated well?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA rules, a licensee must disclose all known material latent defects to a buyer — defects that are not discoverable through reasonable inspection and that could affect the property's value or safety, such as contaminated groundwater or structurally unsound barn foundations. This obligation applies regardless of which party the licensee represents and cannot be waived by the buyer. Sellers are also expected to complete a Property Disclosure Statement that addresses known defects, and buyers should review it carefully alongside independent inspections by qualified professionals familiar with equestrian and rural properties.

Can a buyer use the BC First-Time Home Buyer PTT exemption when purchasing an equestrian property?

The First-Time Home Buyer's Program under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act provides a full PTT exemption for eligible first-time buyers on properties with a fair market value at or below $835,000 (as of 2026), where the property is used as a principal residence. Many equestrian properties — particularly those with extensive acreage, barns, and arenas — will exceed this threshold, meaning the exemption would not apply, or would apply only partially if the value falls within the partial-exemption range. Buyers should consult the BC Ministry of Finance for current eligibility thresholds and confirm whether the agricultural or non-residential portions of the property affect eligibility.

Does the Strata Property Act apply to equestrian properties in BC?

The Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) applies to strata-titled properties, meaning properties subdivided into strata lots with shared common property governed by a strata corporation. The vast majority of equestrian and horse properties in BC are held as fee-simple (non-strata) rural parcels, so the Strata Property Act would not typically apply. However, if a buyer encounters an equestrian property or equestrian facility that is strata-titled — such as a shared arena complex — the Strata Property Act and its regulations would govern matters such as strata bylaws, common property rights, depreciation reports, and contingency reserve funds.

If an equestrian property is part of a deceased owner's estate in BC, what process governs its transfer to beneficiaries or sale?

When an equestrian property forms part of a deceased person's estate in BC, its transfer or sale is governed by the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA, SBC 2009, c. 13), which sets out the rules for probate, executor authority, and distribution of estate assets. The executor named in the will, or an administrator appointed by the court where there is no valid will, has the legal authority to convey title after probate is granted by the BC Supreme Court. Buyers purchasing an estate property should confirm that the executor or administrator has obtained a grant of probate or administration before title can be transferred, and that any ALR or zoning compliance obligations attached to the property are addressed as part of the conveyance.
