Easement

An easement is a registered right granted to another party to use a defined portion of a property for a specific purpose — common examples in BC include utility easements (BC Hydro or gas lines), drainage easements, and shared driveway easements. Easements are registered as charges on title under the Land Title Act and transfer with the property. They can affect where buildings may be located on the burdened parcel and the permitted use of the affected portion. Registered easements appear in a current title search obtained from the Land Title Office or via the LTSA online portal.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an easement and how is it registered in British Columbia?

An easement is a registered right that allows another party to use a defined portion of a property for a specific purpose, such as accessing a utility line or shared driveway. In BC, easements are registered as charges on title under the Land Title Act and are recorded at the Land Title and Survey Authority (LTSA). A current title search obtained through the LTSA online portal will disclose any easements affecting a property.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Does an easement transfer to a new owner when a BC property is sold?

Yes. Because an easement is registered as a charge on title under the Land Title Act, it runs with the land and automatically transfers to any new owner upon sale. The buyer takes the property subject to all registered easements, regardless of whether they were explicitly discussed during negotiations. This is why reviewing a current title search from the LTSA before completing a purchase is essential.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Are BC real estate licensees required to disclose a registered easement to a buyer?

Yes. Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules administered by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), licensees have a duty to disclose all known material latent facts about a property, and a registered easement that materially affects use or value would qualify. A licensee acting for a seller must ensure the buyer is made aware of registered charges, including easements, that could affect the buyer's intended use of the property.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Can a property owner in BC build a structure on top of or within an easement area?

Generally, the terms of the registered easement under the Land Title Act govern what is permitted within the easement area, and most utility or drainage easements prohibit or restrict permanent structures that could interfere with the easement holder's rights. Building within an easement area without authorization from the easement holder can result in a legal requirement to remove the structure at the owner's expense. Buyers should review the registered easement document carefully and consult a BC lawyer before constructing anything within or near the easement boundary.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
How can a buyer in BC find out if a property has an easement registered against it?

A buyer can obtain a current state of title certificate or a title search through the Land Title and Survey Authority (LTSA) online portal, which will list all registered charges, including easements, on a parcel. Easements appear as charges on title under the Land Title Act, and the full easement document can also be retrieved from the LTSA to review the specific terms, location, and permitted uses. Reviewing title early in the transaction helps identify any easements that could affect the buyer's intended use of the property.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Does an easement affect the strata lot or common property in a BC strata development?

Easements can affect strata lots, common property, or limited common property within a strata plan, and are governed by both the Land Title Act and the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43). For example, a utility easement might run across common property, with the strata corporation holding obligations as the burdened party. Buyers should review the Form B Information Certificate and the strata corporation's registered documents to identify any easements disclosed under the Strata Property Act and its Regulation.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Can an easement on BC farmland in the Agricultural Land Reserve create restrictions on farm use?

Yes. An easement registered on land within the Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) can restrict how a portion of the land is used, which may interact with non-farm-use requirements under the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36). The Agricultural Land Commission (ALC) administers restrictions on subdivision and non-farm use of ALR land, and buyers should consider whether a proposed easement or existing easement is consistent with permitted uses under both the easement terms and ALC regulations. Buyers and sellers of ALR land should consult the ALC directly for guidance on how a specific easement may affect permitted agricultural activities.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
Does the existence of an easement affect the Property Transfer Tax payable on a BC property purchase?

The Property Transfer Tax (PTT) under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act is calculated on the fair market value of the property being transferred, and an easement that reduces the utility or fair market value of the land could indirectly affect that assessed value. The PTT tiers of 1% on the first $200,000, 2% on the portion up to $2,000,000, 3% on the portion up to $3,000,000, and an additional 2% on residential value over $3,000,000 are applied to the full transferred interest including any burdened land. Buyers should consult the BC Ministry of Finance or a qualified BC lawyer if they believe a registered easement materially affects the fair market value for PTT purposes.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
How is an easement removed or discharged from title in British Columbia?

In BC, a registered easement can be discharged from title under the Land Title Act by a written release executed by the easement holder, which must then be registered at the LTSA to remove the charge. If the easement holder cannot be located or refuses to release the easement, the burdened property owner may apply to the BC Supreme Court for an order cancelling the easement, for example where it has become spent or its purpose no longer exists. Legal advice from a BC lawyer is advisable for any easement discharge process given the requirements under the Land Title Act.

Verify with: BC Land Title and Survey Authority
If a BC property with an easement is inherited through an estate, does the easement remain on title?

Yes. When a property is transferred through an estate under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA), all registered charges on title, including easements, continue to bind the property and pass to the beneficiary or new owner. The easement runs with the land under the Land Title Act, meaning the change in ownership through inheritance does not extinguish or alter the easement. The executor or administrator of the estate should ensure the beneficiary is fully informed of any registered easements by conducting a current LTSA title search as part of the estate administration process.
