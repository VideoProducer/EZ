Agency & Disclosure

Unrepresented Buyer

A buyer who has chosen not to enter into an agency relationship with a REALTOR® and is therefore not a client of any licensee in the transaction. The listing licensee may interact with an unrepresented buyer but cannot provide advice or advocate on their behalf. Under Section 54 of the Real Estate Services Rules, the licensee must provide the Disclosure of Representation in Trading Services form before any substantive interaction.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 'Unrepresented Buyer' mean in a British Columbia real estate transaction?

An Unrepresented Buyer is a buyer who has chosen not to enter into an agency relationship with any REALTOR® or licensee, meaning no licensee owes them the duties of a client, such as loyalty, confidentiality, or advocacy. Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and its Rules, the listing licensee may still interact with an Unrepresented Buyer to facilitate the transaction but must act solely in the interests of the seller. The buyer in this position is responsible for protecting their own interests throughout the transaction.

Is a listing licensee in BC required to give an Unrepresented Buyer any disclosure documents before speaking with them?

Yes. Under Section 54 of the Real Estate Services Rules made under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA), a listing licensee must provide the Disclosure of Representation in Trading Services form to an Unrepresented Buyer before any substantive discussion about a property. This disclosure explains that the licensee represents the seller and cannot provide advice, recommendations, or advocacy to the buyer. Failure to provide this disclosure before substantive interaction is a breach of the licensee's professional obligations regulated by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA).

Can a listing licensee in BC give advice to an Unrepresented Buyer about whether to make an offer or what price to offer?

No. Because the listing licensee owes fiduciary and agency duties exclusively to the seller, they cannot provide advice, opinions, or advocacy to an Unrepresented Buyer under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA conduct standards. The licensee may provide factual information about the property and the transaction process, but offering strategic guidance — such as suggesting an offer price or recommending contract conditions — would constitute a conflict of interest. An Unrepresented Buyer seeking that level of guidance should engage their own licensed representative.

Does an Unrepresented Buyer in BC still have to pay Property Transfer Tax?

Yes. An Unrepresented Buyer's status has no effect on Property Transfer Tax (PTT) obligations under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act. PTT is calculated on the fair market value of the property at standard rates — currently 1% on the first $200,000, 2% on the portion from $200,001 to $2,000,000, 3% on the portion from $2,000,001 to $3,000,000, and an additional 2% on residential property value above $3,000,000. Eligible first-time buyers may qualify for a full exemption on properties up to $835,000 or a partial exemption up to $860,000 under the First-Time Home Buyers' Program, regardless of whether they are represented.

If an Unrepresented Buyer in BC is purchasing a strata lot, what documents should they be aware of?

When purchasing a strata lot in BC, a buyer should review key documents governed by the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43), including the Form B Information Certificate (which discloses strata fees, special levies, and bylaw enforcement matters), the strata corporation's bylaws and rules, the most recent depreciation report, and financial statements including the contingency reserve fund balance. Although the listing licensee can provide these documents to an Unrepresented Buyer, they cannot advise the buyer on how to interpret them or whether the strata's financial health is adequate. An Unrepresented Buyer bears sole responsibility for reviewing and understanding these materials.

Can an Unrepresented Buyer in BC choose to become a client of the listing licensee during the transaction?

Generally, a listing licensee who is already representing the seller cannot then also represent the buyer as a client in the same transaction without navigating the limited dual agency restrictions under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA rules, which are very narrowly permitted and require specific written disclosure and consent. In most residential transactions, full dual agency — where a single licensee advocates for both buyer and seller — is effectively prohibited under current BCFSA conduct standards. If an Unrepresented Buyer wishes to have representation, they would typically need to engage a different licensee from a different brokerage or, in permitted circumstances, a different licensee within the same brokerage.

How does PIPA apply when a listing licensee in BC collects personal information from an Unrepresented Buyer?

When a listing licensee or their brokerage collects personal information from an Unrepresented Buyer — such as contact details, financial qualification information, or identification for FINTRAC purposes — that collection is governed by BC's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA). Under PIPA, personal information may only be collected for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate, and the individual must be informed of the purpose of collection. The brokerage must also protect that information from unauthorized access or disclosure, even though the buyer is not a client of the brokerage.

Does an Unrepresented Buyer in BC have any recourse if they believe a listing licensee acted improperly toward them?

Yes. Even though the listing licensee does not represent the Unrepresented Buyer, all licensees in BC have obligations under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and BCFSA conduct standards to treat all parties to a transaction with honesty and to not engage in conduct that is fraudulent, deceptive, or in bad faith. An Unrepresented Buyer who believes a licensee has breached these statutory duties may file a complaint with the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), which has the authority to investigate and discipline licensees. Civil remedies through the courts may also be available depending on the circumstances.

Does an Unrepresented Buyer in BC save money on commission by not having their own agent?

Not necessarily in a direct, guaranteed way. In BC, commission structures are negotiated between the seller and the listing brokerage, and any commission or remuneration paid in a transaction is governed by the terms of the listing agreement under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA). Whether the seller's brokerage retains all of the commission or passes any savings to the seller when there is no buyer's licensee depends entirely on the terms negotiated between the seller and their brokerage — the Unrepresented Buyer has no contractual entitlement to any portion of that commission. Buyers should clarify all financial arrangements directly with the seller, as the listing licensee cannot provide the buyer with advice on this matter.

If an Unrepresented Buyer in BC is purchasing a property within the Agricultural Land Reserve, are there any special considerations they should know about?

Yes. Purchasing property within the Agricultural Land Reserve (ALR) in BC brings significant land-use restrictions regulated by the Agricultural Land Commission Act (SBC 2002, c. 36) and administered by the Agricultural Land Commission (ALC). Uses of ALR land are restricted to farm use and limited residential use as defined in the Act and ALC regulations, and non-farm use or subdivision typically requires ALC approval. Because a listing licensee cannot advise an Unrepresented Buyer, the buyer bears sole responsibility for investigating these restrictions, and should consult the ALC directly or obtain independent legal advice to understand what uses are permitted on the specific parcel.
