Home Inspection

A visual examination of the home's major systems and components — foundation, roof, plumbing, electrical, heating, insulation, and more — conducted by a licensed home inspector. Under the Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act and the Home Inspector Licensing Regulation (BC Reg 12/2009), home inspectors must be licensed by Consumer Protection BC. A home inspection typically costs $400-$600 and takes 2-3 hours. A subject-to-inspection clause is the standard mechanism for incorporating an inspection into an offer. The inspection report supports the buyer's understanding of the home's condition and informs any negotiation over repairs or price adjustments.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a home inspector in British Columbia required to be licensed?

Yes. Under the Home Inspector Licensing Regulation (BC Reg 12/2009), made pursuant to the Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act, all home inspectors offering services in BC must hold a valid licence issued by Consumer Protection BC. Engaging an unlicensed home inspector is a violation of that regulatory framework, and buyers should verify a inspector's licence status directly with Consumer Protection BC before booking.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
What does a home inspection in BC typically examine?

A licensed home inspector conducts a visual examination of the home's major systems and components, including the foundation, roof, plumbing, electrical system, heating and cooling systems, insulation, and windows and doors. The inspection is non-invasive and non-destructive, meaning the inspector observes accessible areas rather than opening walls or excavating. The resulting written report documents observed conditions and any deficiencies noted at the time of inspection.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
How much does a home inspection typically cost in British Columbia?

Home inspection fees in BC typically range from approximately $400 to $600, though costs can vary depending on the size, age, and complexity of the property, as well as the inspector's experience and the region. Larger or older homes, or those with additional systems such as septic or well water, may attract higher fees. Buyers should obtain a written quote from the inspector before confirming the appointment.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
What is a subject-to-inspection clause in a BC real estate contract?

A subject-to-inspection clause is a condition written into an offer to purchase that makes the buyer's obligation to complete the transaction contingent on the results of a home inspection being satisfactory to the buyer. If the inspection reveals issues the buyer cannot accept, the buyer may be entitled to remove the subject, renegotiate terms, or walk away from the contract, depending on how the clause is drafted. Buyers and sellers should ensure the clause clearly specifies the deadline for removal and the standard of satisfaction that applies.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
Does a BC real estate licensee have any duties relating to a buyer's decision to waive a home inspection?

Under the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the rules set by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), a licensee acting as a buyer's agent owes fiduciary and statutory duties that include acting in the buyer's best interests and providing them with information relevant to the transaction. While a buyer may ultimately choose to waive an inspection condition, a licensee should ensure the buyer is fully informed of the risks of doing so before removing the subject. BCFSA guidance on buyer representation sets out these professional obligations in detail.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
Can a home inspection be used to renegotiate the purchase price in BC?

Yes. If a home inspection reveals material deficiencies, a buyer may use the findings as a basis to negotiate a price reduction, request that the seller complete repairs before closing, or seek a credit in lieu of repairs, provided the parties mutually agree to any amendment to the contract. Neither party is legally obligated to renegotiate, and the outcome depends on the terms of the subject clause and the willingness of both sides. Any agreed-upon amendment to the original contract of purchase and sale should be documented in writing.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
Does a strata lot in BC require a different approach to home inspection compared to a freehold property?

When purchasing a strata lot in BC, a home inspection typically covers only the interior of the individual unit and any limited common property assigned to it, since the strata corporation is responsible for common property and the building envelope under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43). Buyers are also encouraged to review the depreciation report (if the strata corporation has one), Form B Information Certificate, meeting minutes, and financial statements to understand the condition and financial health of the strata corporation as a whole. Combining a unit inspection with a review of strata documents provides a more complete picture of the property's condition.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
Is a seller in BC required to disclose known defects even if a buyer waives a home inspection?

Yes. In BC, sellers have a common law and professional obligation (when represented by a licensee under RESA) to disclose known material latent defects — defects that are not visible on a reasonable inspection and that could affect the property's value or safety. Waiving a home inspection does not relieve the seller of this disclosure duty, nor does it extinguish any legal remedies a buyer may have if a known material latent defect was fraudulently concealed. Patent defects (those visible on a reasonable inspection) are generally the buyer's responsibility to discover.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
What recourse does a BC buyer have if a licensed home inspector misses a significant defect?

If a buyer believes a licensed home inspector was negligent in failing to identify a significant defect, the buyer may have a civil claim in negligence against the inspector, subject to applicable limitation periods under BC law. Complaints about a licensee's conduct may also be filed with Consumer Protection BC, which has authority to investigate and discipline licensed home inspectors under the Business Practices and Consumer Protection Act and the Home Inspector Licensing Regulation (BC Reg 12/2009). The scope of the inspector's liability is also shaped by the terms of the inspection contract, which buyers should review carefully before signing.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
Does privacy legislation in BC affect how a home inspector handles the inspection report?

Yes. A home inspection report contains information about a property and may incidentally capture personal information about its occupants; the collection, use, and disclosure of any such personal information by a home inspector operating as a private-sector organization in BC is governed by the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA). The report itself is typically prepared for the buyer who commissioned it, and sharing it with third parties without appropriate authorization could raise PIPA compliance concerns. Buyers and inspectors should discuss the intended distribution of the report at the outset of the engagement.

Verify with: Consumer Protection BC
