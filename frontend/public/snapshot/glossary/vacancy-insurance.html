Vacancy Insurance

Vacancy insurance is specialized coverage for homes that are unoccupied for extended periods — typically 30 days or more. Standard home insurance policies exclude or limit coverage when a home is vacant, with water damage, vandalism, and theft commonly excluded after the vacancy threshold is reached. Vacancy coverage is relevant for estate sales, snowbird absences, and properties between tenants. Insurers require notification before extended vacancy and add the appropriate endorsement under the policy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is vacancy insurance and why does it matter for BC homeowners?

Vacancy insurance is a specialized policy endorsement that maintains coverage for a home that has been unoccupied for an extended period — typically 30 consecutive days or more — after standard home insurance protections would otherwise lapse or be significantly reduced. In British Columbia, standard residential policies commonly exclude water damage, vandalism, and theft once a vacancy threshold is crossed, leaving owners financially exposed. Homeowners in BC should notify their insurer before an extended absence and request the appropriate vacancy endorsement to avoid uninsured losses.

How does vacancy insurance apply to a BC estate property being held for sale after an owner has died?

When a BC homeowner dies, the executor or personal representative appointed under the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA) takes on responsibility for managing and protecting estate assets, which includes maintaining adequate insurance on any real property. If the home sits vacant during the probate process or while awaiting sale, the standard home insurance policy may reduce or exclude coverage once the vacancy threshold is reached. The executor should promptly notify the insurer of the vacancy and arrange a vacancy endorsement to ensure the estate's property remains properly insured throughout the administration period.

Does a strata lot owner in BC need vacancy insurance if their unit is empty for several months?

Under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43), a strata corporation is required to obtain insurance for the common property and buildings, but that master policy does not cover a unit owner's personal contents or improvements, nor does it address the owner's liability arising from their individual unit. If a strata lot is vacant for an extended period, the owner's individual unit policy may exclude or limit coverage under its standard vacancy clause, just as a freehold policy would. Strata lot owners should review their individual unit policy and arrange a vacancy endorsement with their insurer if the unit will be unoccupied beyond the policy's vacancy threshold.

What is the typical vacancy threshold in BC home insurance policies and who sets it?

While there is no BC statute that legislates a universal vacancy threshold for private insurance policies, most BC insurers apply a threshold of 30 consecutive days of unoccupancy before vacancy exclusions take effect, though some policies use different timeframes. The specific threshold, definitions of 'vacancy,' and available endorsements are determined by the individual insurer and policy wording rather than by any government regulation. BC homeowners should review their specific policy documents and confirm the exact threshold with their insurer before leaving a property unoccupied.

How does vacancy insurance relate to BC's Speculation and Vacancy Tax?

The BC Speculation and Vacancy Tax, administered under provincial legislation and overseen by the BC Ministry of Finance, applies to residential properties in designated areas that are not used as a principal residence and are not rented out for a minimum period during the calendar year. Vacancy insurance is a separate, private insurance matter and does not affect or satisfy any obligation under the Speculation and Vacancy Tax. Owners of vacant properties in designated areas should consult the BC Ministry of Finance for current exemption thresholds and filing requirements, as the tax and insurance obligations operate entirely independently.

Are BC real estate licensees required to advise clients about vacancy insurance?

BC real estate licensees are governed by the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and its Rules as administered by the British Columbia Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), which require licensees to act in the best interests of their clients and to disclose material information relevant to a trade. While RESA does not explicitly enumerate vacancy insurance as a mandatory disclosure item, a licensee aware that a property will be vacant — such as during a lengthy listing period — may reasonably bring the issue to a client's attention as part of fulfilling their duty of competence and care. Licensees should not provide insurance advice themselves but can recommend that the client contact their insurer.

Can a BC landlord's standard rental property insurance cover a suite that is vacant between tenants?

Standard landlord or rental property insurance policies in BC often contain vacancy clauses that reduce or suspend certain coverages — such as protection against water damage, vandalism, or glass breakage — once the property has been unoccupied for the insurer's specified threshold period, commonly around 30 days. During the period between tenants, landlords should notify their insurer of the vacancy and enquire about adding a vacancy endorsement to maintain broader coverage. The applicable threshold and available endorsements vary by insurer and policy, so landlords should confirm the details directly with their insurance provider.

Does vacancy insurance cover a BC property owned by a non-resident or foreign owner?

Vacancy insurance is available to any property owner in BC, regardless of residency or nationality, subject to an insurer's underwriting criteria and eligibility requirements. However, non-resident owners should be aware that holding a vacant residential property in BC may also attract obligations under the BC Speculation and Vacancy Tax and, depending on the transaction history, the Additional Property Transfer Tax provisions under the BC Property Transfer Tax Act — these are entirely separate from insurance matters. Non-resident owners should coordinate with both a licensed BC insurer and qualified legal and tax advisors to address all applicable obligations.

How should a 'snowbird' BC homeowner handle insurance when they leave for an extended winter absence?

BC homeowners who spend extended periods outside the province — commonly referred to as 'snowbirds' — should notify their home insurer before departing, as most standard policies begin to restrict or exclude key coverages such as water damage and vandalism once the home is unoccupied beyond the policy's vacancy threshold, which is frequently around 30 consecutive days. The insurer can typically add a vacancy endorsement that, subject to specific conditions such as regular property inspections, maintains broader coverage during the absence. The exact conditions, inspection requirements, and any premium adjustments vary by insurer and policy, so homeowners should confirm the terms in writing before leaving.

If a BC property is vacant and suffers a water damage loss, what happens if the owner did not have vacancy insurance?

If a BC homeowner's standard policy contained a vacancy exclusion and the owner failed to notify the insurer or obtain a vacancy endorsement, the insurer may deny the water damage claim on the basis that coverage was suspended or excluded once the vacancy threshold was reached. The outcome depends on the specific policy wording, the insurer's position, and whether any applicable BC insurance legislation — including the Insurance Act of British Columbia — provides any consumer protections regarding the denial. Homeowners who believe a claim has been improperly denied should contact their insurer in writing, and may also seek guidance from the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) or a licensed insurance adjuster regarding the dispute process.
