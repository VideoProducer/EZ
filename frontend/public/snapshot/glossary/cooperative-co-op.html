Title & Ownership

Cooperative (Co-op)

A housing cooperative is a form of ownership where residents own shares in a corporation that owns the building, rather than owning a strata lot directly. Shareholders typically have an exclusive right to occupy a specific unit. Co-ops have unique financing, transfer, and approval processes that differ materially from strata or fee simple ownership.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a buyer actually own when they purchase a co-op in British Columbia?

In a BC housing cooperative, a buyer does not acquire title to real property in the traditional sense; instead, they purchase shares in a cooperative corporation that holds title to the entire building or land. Those shares come with a proprietary lease or occupancy agreement granting the shareholder an exclusive right to occupy a specific unit. Because no real property changes hands in the conventional way, the transaction is fundamentally different from purchasing a strata lot under the Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) or a fee simple property.

Is a BC co-op unit governed by the Strata Property Act?

No. The Strata Property Act (SBC 1998, c. 43) governs strata corporations and the ownership of strata lots and common property, but a housing cooperative is a separate legal structure in which a corporation—not individual owners—holds title to the building. Co-ops are instead governed by the cooperative's own constating documents (articles, rules, and bylaws) and, where applicable, the BC Cooperative Association Act. Buyers should review the cooperative's governing documents carefully, as there is no equivalent to the Strata Property Act's consumer-protection framework applying to co-ops.

Does Property Transfer Tax apply when purchasing shares in a BC co-op?

The BC Property Transfer Tax Act applies to transfers of a 'taxable transaction,' which is generally defined as a transfer of an interest in land; because a co-op share purchase typically transfers shares in a corporation rather than an interest in land, it may fall outside the standard PTT regime. However, if the transaction is structured in a way that results in a transfer of a beneficial interest in land, PTT could apply, and specific exemptions such as the First-Time Home Buyers' Program (full exemption up to $835,000) may not be available in the same way they are for fee simple or strata purchases. Buyers should seek advice from a BC lawyer and consult the BC Ministry of Finance to confirm the PTT treatment for their specific co-op transaction.

Why do many BC lenders decline to finance co-op purchases, and what financing options exist?

Most major institutional lenders in BC are reluctant to provide mortgage financing for co-op units because the buyer receives shares rather than title to real property, meaning the lender cannot register a conventional mortgage against land under the Land Title Act. Some credit unions and specialized lenders offer share-secured loans, but these products typically carry different terms, higher interest rates, or lower loan-to-value ratios than conventional mortgages. Prospective buyers should consult lenders experienced with cooperative housing before making an offer, as financing conditions for co-ops differ materially from those for strata or fee simple purchases.

What is the board approval process for transferring a co-op unit in BC, and can a board reject a buyer?

Most BC housing cooperatives require that the board of directors approve any incoming shareholder before a share transfer is completed, and the cooperative's bylaws typically set out the grounds and process for such approval. A board may reject a prospective buyer for reasons permitted under the bylaws—such as failure to meet financial criteria or failure to complete an interview process—provided the grounds do not violate the BC Human Rights Code. Unlike a strata corporation, which has limited ability to restrict resale under the Strata Property Act, a cooperative's board approval requirement is a defining feature of the co-op ownership model.

What disclosure obligations does a BC real estate licensee have when representing a party in a co-op transaction?

BC real estate licensees are governed by the Real Estate Services Act (RESA) and the Rules made under it, administered by the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA), and must disclose all known material latent defects, conflicts of interest, and the nature of their agency relationship to clients and unrepresented parties. Because co-op transactions involve shares rather than land, licensees must ensure they fully understand and disclose the unique characteristics of co-op ownership—including board approval requirements, financing limitations, and the absence of Strata Property Act protections—so that clients can make informed decisions. Licensees who are uncertain whether a particular co-op transaction falls within their licensing scope under RESA should consult BCFSA guidance.

How does the Speculation and Vacancy Tax (SVT) interact with co-op units in BC?

The BC Speculation and Vacancy Tax applies to residential properties in designated areas of BC, and its application to co-op units depends on how the cooperative's interest in land is characterized under the relevant provincial legislation. Because co-op residents hold shares rather than registered title, the tax treatment may differ from that of fee simple or strata owners, and the cooperative corporation itself may be the assessed party. Owners or potential buyers of co-op units in SVT-applicable areas should consult the BC Ministry of Finance and a qualified tax professional to determine their specific obligations.

What happens to a deceased person's co-op shares in BC, and how does WESA apply?

When a BC co-op shareholder dies, their shares form part of their estate and are distributed according to their will or, if there is no will, according to the intestacy provisions of the Wills, Estates and Succession Act (WESA, SBC 2009, c. 13). However, a transfer of shares to a beneficiary or purchaser from the estate is still subject to the cooperative's board approval process as set out in the co-op's bylaws. Executors and administrators should review the co-op's governing documents alongside the requirements of WESA to understand both their estate-administration obligations and the cooperative's consent requirements.

Can a co-op shareholder in BC rent out their unit to a third party?

The right to sublet or rent a co-op unit in BC is governed entirely by the cooperative's own bylaws and the shareholder's occupancy agreement, not by the Strata Property Act (which applies to strata corporations, not co-ops). Many cooperatives restrict or prohibit subletting in order to maintain the owner-occupancy character of the community, and doing so without board approval may constitute a breach of the occupancy agreement and grounds for termination of the shareholder's rights. Shareholders should review their cooperative's rules carefully before entering into any tenancy arrangement.

Verify with: Cooperative Housing
How is personal information about co-op applicants and shareholders protected in BC?

Personal information collected by a BC cooperative corporation—such as financial records, identification, and application details gathered during the board approval process—is subject to the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA, SBC 2003, c. 63), which requires that organizations collect, use, and disclose personal information only with consent and for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate. Real estate licensees involved in co-op transactions who handle personal information about buyers or sellers are also bound by PIPA and, where applicable, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) for any commercial electronic communications. Both the cooperative and any licensees involved should have clear privacy policies and obtain proper consent before collecting or sharing personal information.
